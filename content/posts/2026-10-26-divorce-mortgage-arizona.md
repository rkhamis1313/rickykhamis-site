The single most common misunderstanding in this situation: **a divorce decree does not remove anyone from a mortgage.**

A decree is an order between the parties. The mortgage is a contract with a lender who was not party to the divorce and is not bound by it. If both names are on the note, both remain liable to that lender until the loan is paid off or replaced, no matter what the decree says about who gets the house.

Everything else follows from that.

## The three ways a name actually comes off

**1. Refinance into one name.** The usual path. The spouse keeping the house obtains a new loan in their name alone, which pays off the joint loan. The other spouse is released because the loan they signed no longer exists.

The requirement: **the remaining spouse has to qualify on their own.** Their income, their credit, their ratio, against the full payment. That is the real constraint, and it is why this does not always work.

**2. Sell the house.** The loan is paid off at closing and both are released. Clean, and sometimes the only realistic option.

**3. Loan assumption, where the loan permits it.** Some loans are assumable, and some assumption processes allow a release of liability for the departing spouse. This is worth asking about specifically because it can preserve a below-market rate, which in this market is a genuinely valuable asset. It is not available on most conventional loans, and where it is available the remaining spouse still generally has to qualify. Ask the servicer by name about your specific loan.

A quitclaim deed is not on this list on purpose. **A quitclaim deed changes title, not the loan.** A spouse who signs a quitclaim has given up ownership and remains liable on the debt, which is the worst of both positions. It has its place as part of a properly structured transaction; it is not a solution by itself.

## Qualifying on your own

The spouse keeping the house needs to carry it alone in an underwriter's arithmetic.

**Income.** Employment or self-employment income as usual. Then:

**Spousal maintenance or child support can count as income**, with requirements. Generally the award must be documented in the decree, there must be evidence of actual receipt for a period, and there must be a defined remaining duration, since income that ends soon is worth little for qualifying a thirty year loan. Requirements vary by program.

**Obligations you pay can count against you.** Court-ordered support you are paying is a liability in your ratio.

**Your credit.** If joint accounts went unpaid during the separation, the damage is on both reports. Late payments on a joint mortgage harm the departing spouse as much as the one who stayed, which is worth both parties understanding while the separation is in progress rather than after.

**The equity buyout.** If you are paying your former spouse their share, that money has to come from somewhere, usually from the new loan. Which makes this a cash-out refinance, with cash-out loan-to-value limits and pricing, unless a specific exception applies.

That last point is important and frequently decisive. Some programs treat a buyout pursuant to a divorce decree more favorably than a standard cash-out refinance, sometimes allowing a higher loan-to-value. Whether it applies depends on the program and on the decree's language. **Ask about this before the decree is finalized**, because the way the agreement is written can affect which treatment is available. Your attorney drafts the decree; your lender can tell them what financing treatment needs in order to work. Getting those two in the same conversation early saves real money.

## The Arizona community property wrinkle

Arizona is a community property state, and that affects several things here in ways that differ from most of the country:

**Debts incurred during the marriage are generally community debts**, which affects liability regardless of whose name appears where.

**A spouse's signature may be required on documents** even where that spouse is not a borrower, specifically to address community property interests. A non-borrowing spouse signing certain documents at closing is routine in Arizona and does not make them liable on the note.

**Title vesting matters** and the options available to a married person, a single person, and parties taking title together differ. Community property with right of survivorship, joint tenancy and sole and separate property have different consequences.

**Taking title as sole and separate property** generally requires a disclaimer deed from the other spouse. If you are buying during or after a separation, raise this with your lender and your attorney early, because escrow will need it and discovering the requirement late costs days.

None of this is legal advice and none of it is my lane. It is a list of things to raise with your attorney, and the reason to raise them with your lender too is that they affect how the loan must be structured.

## If you are buying rather than keeping

**Your old mortgage counts against you until you are off it.** If you are still on the loan for the marital home, that payment is generally in your ratio when you buy, unless it is excluded under specific program conditions.

**Ask about those conditions specifically.** Program treatment of a mortgage assigned to a former spouse by a decree varies, and some programs will exclude the payment where the decree assigns responsibility and there is evidence the other party is paying it. That is a meaningful difference between investors and a real reason to shop rather than accept the first answer. See [Rental Income From a Departing Residence](/blog/rental-income-departing-residence/) for the adjacent problem.

**Support you receive can be qualifying income**, with the documentation requirements above.

**Rebuild the credit first if it took damage.** Credit work done before you shop changes which programs exist for you. Done after you are in contract it changes nothing, because the timeline will not allow it. See [Fix the Credit First](/blog/credit-repair-before-mortgage/).

## The sequence that works

1. **Talk to a lender before the decree is final.** The single most valuable thing on this page. The decree's language about the house, the buyout and support can determine which financing treatments are available.
2. **Find out whether the remaining spouse can qualify alone.** If they cannot, the plan to keep the house is not a plan, and knowing that early changes the negotiation.
3. **Ask whether the existing loan is assumable**, since preserving a below-market rate may be worth more than anything else on the table.
4. **Keep every joint account current** through the separation. Damage here hurts both parties.
5. **Get the support documentation in order** if it will be used as income.
6. **Do not rely on a quitclaim deed** to remove anyone from a loan. It does not.
7. **Get fully underwritten** rather than pre-qualified, so the answer is real. See [Pre-Qualified, Pre-Approved, Underwritten](/blog/underwritten-preapproval-vs-prequalification/).
