Every buyer who hears about a temporary buydown asks some version of the same question. What happens when it runs out?

The answer is simple and it should be given immediately. The payment steps up to the note rate and stays there for the rest of the loan.

A buyer who hears that in the kitchen at an open house evaluates it calmly. A buyer who discovers it in underwriting walks, and tells people why. The difference is entirely in when it gets said.

## The schedule, stated plainly

On a 2-1 buydown: twelve months at two points below the note rate, twelve months at one point below, then the note rate from month twenty five onward. Permanently.

On a 3-2-1: three points below, then two, then one, then the note rate from month thirty seven.

Nothing adjusts. The loan was a fixed rate mortgage the entire time. The subsidy simply runs out.

## The three honest answers to the objection

**1. The buyer was underwritten at the full note rate.**

This is the strongest answer and the one most agents do not know to give.

On most programs the underwriter tests the buyer's debt-to-income against the note rate payment, not the subsidized one. So a buyer who was approved was approved at the number they will eventually pay. The buydown is not stretching them into a house they cannot afford. It is a cash flow cushion layered on top of an approval that already works at the higher payment.

Two sentences, and the objection is mostly gone:

> You are being approved at the full payment, not the first year payment. The buydown is extra breathing room in the early years, not the reason you qualify.

The exception is a program specifically designed to qualify the buyer at the reduced rate, which is what [List & Lock](https://www.epiqlending.com/mysite/Ricky-Khamis/listnlock) does. That is a different and in some ways better situation, and it should be explained as what it is rather than conflated with a standard buydown.

**2. The early years are the expensive years.**

The first eighteen months of owning a house are when money leaves fastest. Moving, window coverings, a refrigerator that does not come with the house, the landscaping the inspection flagged, the water heater that turns out to be fourteen years old.

Payment relief concentrated in exactly that window has real value even if nothing about the buyer's income changes. It is not a trick. It is relief where the pressure is.

**3. If rates fall, the buyer refinances. If they do not, the buyer pays a payment they already qualified for.**

Note the order of that sentence. The downside case comes second and it is survivable. That is what makes it an honest argument.

## What not to say

**Do not say "you will just refinance before then."** Nobody knows where rates go. A buyer who bought on that assumption and finds rates unchanged in year three feels lied to, correctly. Refinancing is an upside scenario, never the plan.

**Do not minimize the step up.** It is a real increase and the buyer can do arithmetic. Saying the number out loud builds more trust than softening it.

**Do not quote the first year payment without the later ones.** In marketing, in conversation, anywhere. The full ladder, every time. See [Advertising a Payment Without Creating a Compliance Problem](/blog/open-house-payment-flyer-compliance/).

## What a buyer should actually check before accepting one

**Confirm what rate you are being qualified at.** Ask directly. If the answer is the note rate, look at that payment and decide whether you are comfortable with it, because that is your payment from year three forward.

**Look at the note rate payment against your real budget**, not your pre-approval maximum. Those are different numbers.

**Ask what happens to unused escrow funds** if you sell or refinance during the subsidy period. Generally applied to the payoff rather than refunded. Terms vary.

**Ask whether the same money could do more as a permanent reduction.** If you intend to hold the loan a long time, it frequently can. See [Permanent vs Temporary](/blog/permanent-vs-temporary-buydown/).

**Confirm your reserves survive the structure.** Do not fund a buydown with money the loan requires you to still have after closing.

## Why this post exists

Because the step up is the single most common reason a buydown falls apart, and it almost never falls apart because of the economics. It falls apart because somebody did not mention it early and the buyer concluded that if this was not disclosed, something else was not either.

Say it first. Say the number. Then explain why it works anyway.
