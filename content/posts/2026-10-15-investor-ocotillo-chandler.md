Ocotillo occupies the southwest part of Chandler, built around a series of man-made lakes and the golf course that threads between them. The rental thesis is straightforward and it is not about the water: Chandler's employment base includes substantial semiconductor and technology operations, and the tenant pool that produces tends to be credit-worthy, relocation-driven and willing to pay for a well-located house.

If you are buying there as an investor, the financing question is whether to qualify on your income or on the property's.

## What a DSCR loan does

A DSCR loan, for debt service coverage ratio, sizes the loan off the property's rental income rather than your personal income. No tax returns, no W-2s, no debt-to-income calculation on you.

The ratio is simple: the property's gross rent divided by its total monthly payment including principal, interest, taxes, insurance and association dues. Above 1.00 means the rent covers the payment. Program minimums vary, and a stronger ratio generally produces better pricing. See [How to Calculate DSCR](/blog/how-to-calculate-dscr/).

For whom this is the right tool:

- **Self-employed investors** whose returns show aggressive deductions. The returns are irrelevant here.
- **Investors who already own several properties** and have run into the limits of conventional investor financing. See [DSCR vs Conventional Investment Financing](/blog/dscr-vs-conventional-investment/).
- **Anyone buying in an entity.** DSCR loans routinely vest in an LLC, which conventional financing generally does not. See [DSCR Loans and LLC Vesting](/blog/dscr-llc-vesting/).

What it still requires: credit, down payment, and reserves. A DSCR loan is not a no-qualifying loan, it is a differently-qualifying loan. See [DSCR Credit and Reserve Requirements](/blog/dscr-credit-reserves-requirements/).

## Running the numbers honestly on an Ocotillo property

The ratio is only as good as its inputs, and investors routinely flatter all three.

**Rent.** The underwriter uses a market rent determination from the appraiser, generally on Form 1007, not your pro forma. If the appraiser's number comes in below your assumption, your ratio drops and the loan may resize. Get a realistic rent estimate from someone who manages property in the specific area before you write.

**Association dues.** These go into the payment side of the ratio, which means they directly reduce how much you can borrow. Ocotillo's community structure means dues are a real line item, and some properties sit inside sub-associations with their own additional obligation. Get the complete figure for the specific address rather than the community average.

**Taxes and insurance.** Use the real assessed amount, not the seller's number if an exemption or a reassessment is in play. Insurance on a property backing a lake or a golf course is worth pricing specifically rather than estimating.

And one Ocotillo-specific item: **ask what the property's obligations are with respect to the lakes.** Waterfront and lake-adjacent properties in planned lake communities sometimes carry additional assessments, maintenance responsibilities, or rules about docks and shoreline. All of it belongs in the ratio or in your reserve planning.

## Occupancy and rental strategy

**Long term rental** is the straightforward case and what most DSCR programs are built around.

**Short term rental** is a different conversation. Chandler regulates short term rentals under the framework Arizona cities operate within, including licensing and tax registration requirements, and association rules frequently restrict it independently of what the city permits. In a community like Ocotillo, the association's position may be the binding constraint. Confirm both the municipal requirements and the association's governing documents before you build a model on nightly rates. Some DSCR programs will underwrite short term rental income and some will not. See [Short-Term Rental DSCR Loans](/blog/short-term-rental-dscr-scottsdale/).

**Do not finance it as a second home and rent it.** Occupancy misrepresentation is loan fraud. If it is a rental, finance it as one. See [Second Home vs Investment](/blog/scottsdale-second-home-vs-investment/).

## Where these files actually go sideways

**The appraiser's market rent comes in low.** Most common failure. Build cushion into your assumed ratio.

**Dues were underestimated.** Second most common, and entirely preventable.

**Reserves were spent on the down payment.** Reserve requirements are tested after closing and on a portfolio they frequently scale with the number of financed properties. Do not allocate your last liquid dollar to the purchase.

**Entity documents are not ready.** If vesting in an LLC, the operating agreement, articles and certificate of good standing need to be in order before closing, not during.

**The condominium or project fails review.** If you are buying attached product, project eligibility applies to investor loans too, and investor concentration limits are precisely the thing that trips up a building full of rentals.

## Before you write in Ocotillo

- **Get a realistic market rent** from someone who manages in the area.
- **Get the complete dues obligation** including any sub-association and any lake-related assessment.
- **Confirm the short term rental position** of both the city and the association, if that is your plan.
- **Calculate the ratio with real taxes and real insurance** before you make an offer.
- **Have your entity documents ready** if vesting in an LLC.
- **Confirm reserves after closing**, at the requirement that applies to your total financed property count.
