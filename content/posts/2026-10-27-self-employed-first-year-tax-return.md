The standard answer is two years. You have one. Here is what is actually available, because the two year rule is a default rather than a wall.

## Why two years is the default

Underwriting wants evidence that income is stable and likely to continue. Two years of filed returns provides a trend: a second year at or above the first suggests stability, and a second year below the first suggests a direction, which is why **a declining second year generally becomes your qualifying income rather than the average.**

One year provides a data point with no trend. The question underwriting asks is whether other evidence can substitute for the missing year.

Sometimes it can.

## The agency exception, and what it actually requires

Conventional guidelines do contemplate a self-employed borrower with one year of filed returns, and the requirements are specific. Expect the underwriter to be looking for:

**A full year of filed returns** for the business, covering a complete tax year rather than a partial one.

**Documented experience in the same line of work** before you went out on your own. This is the heart of it. Someone who spent eight years as a W-2 employee in an industry and then started their own firm in that industry is a materially different risk from someone who changed careers entirely. The underwriter wants evidence of the prior experience: W-2s, employment verification, licenses, a resume that the documents support.

**Evidence the business is stable or growing**, which may include year-to-date profit and loss statements, business bank statements, and the trajectory those show.

**A strong overall file.** These approvals favor good credit, meaningful reserves and a comfortable ratio. The exception exists for strong files, not as a general accommodation.

Program guidelines and investor overlays differ, so confirm against the specific program. The practical point is that **it is worth asking rather than assuming you must wait**, particularly if you came from the same industry.

## What works when the agency route does not

**Bank statement programs.** The most useful tool here, because they look at deposits rather than filed returns. Twelve months of statements is a common structure and twelve months is something a one-year-old business has.

How it works: deposits are totaled, internal transfers between your own accounts are stripped out, and an expense factor is applied based on business type to arrive at a qualifying income. The expense factor matters enormously, and it varies by the nature of the business. See [How Bank Statement Income Is Calculated](/blog/bank-statement-loans-how-income-is-calculated/), [12 vs 24 Month Bank Statements](/blog/12-vs-24-month-bank-statements/) and [Expense Factors by Business Type](/blog/expense-factor-by-business-type/).

Two practical notes. Keep business and personal banking separate from the day you start, because commingled accounts make this analysis harder and sometimes impossible. And understand that large unusual deposits will be questioned and may be excluded. See [Large Deposits and NSF Items](/blog/large-deposits-nsf-bank-statements/) and [Personal vs Business Bank Statements](/blog/personal-vs-business-bank-statements/).

**Profit and loss programs.** A CPA-prepared profit and loss statement, generally supported by bank statements. See [P&L and WVOE Loans](/blog/profit-and-loss-wvoe-loans-self-employed/).

**Asset depletion.** If you have substantial liquid assets, qualifying income can be derived from them rather than from your business at all. Your one year of self-employment becomes largely irrelevant. See [Asset Depletion and Asset Qualifier Loans](/blog/asset-depletion-asset-qualifier-loans/).

**No-ratio.** No debt-to-income test at all. Credit, assets, reserves and the property carry the file. This is the structure that leans hardest on credit, which is why credit work before you shop is worth more to a self-employed borrower than to a salaried one. See [Fix the Credit First](/blog/credit-repair-before-mortgage/).

**DSCR, if you are buying an investment property.** Qualifies on the property's rent, not your income. Your employment history does not enter into it. See [DSCR Loans for Scottsdale Investors](/blog/dscr-loans-scottsdale-investors/).

**A co-borrower with W-2 income**, which solves an income shortfall and not much else. See [Co-Signer or Non-Occupant Co-Borrower](/blog/co-signer-vs-non-occupant-coborrower/).

## The mistake to avoid right now

If you are in your first or second year of self-employment and plan to buy, **talk to your CPA and your lender together before you file.**

Here is why. Aggressive deductions reduce your tax bill and reduce your qualifying income. On an agency file, each dollar of deduction costs roughly four to five dollars of buying power. A return filed to minimize tax can cost you the house, and once it is filed, it is filed.

That does not mean overstating income. It means understanding, before the return is finalized, what the trade-off actually is in dollars of purchasing power, and making the decision knowingly. Sometimes the deduction is worth more than the house. Frequently it is not, and almost nobody runs the comparison.

See [Business Owner Write-Offs and Your Mortgage](/blog/business-owner-write-offs-mortgage/).

## If the business is really a continuation

One situation worth flagging because it gets handled badly. A borrower who was a W-2 employee of a firm and now does the same work as a 1099 contractor or through their own entity, for some of the same clients, is in a stronger position than a true startup. The income has continuity even though the tax treatment changed.

Document that continuity explicitly: prior W-2s, the nature of the work before and after, client relationships that carried over, licenses held throughout. An underwriter can only credit a history they can see. See [1099 Contractor and Gig Income](/blog/1099-contractor-gig-mortgage/) and [Recently Self-Employed](/blog/recently-self-employed-mortgage/).

## What to do in the meantime if nothing fits yet

**Keep immaculate banking.** Business account separate from personal, from today. This single habit determines whether a bank statement program will work for you in twelve months.

**Build reserves.** Every alternative structure above leans on reserves. They are tested after closing and they are frequently what makes a thin file approvable.

**Work on credit.** It is doing more work on a non-QM file than on an agency file, so it is worth more to you. See [Fix the Credit First](/blog/credit-repair-before-mortgage/).

**Do not take on new debt.** Ratio room is scarce when income is being rebuilt downward.

**Get underwritten now, even if you are not buying yet.** Find out your real number and the real gap. A plan built on an underwriter's figure is a plan; a plan built on what your business feels like it earns is a guess. See [Pre-Qualified, Pre-Approved, Underwritten](/blog/underwritten-preapproval-vs-prequalification/).

## Before you shop

- **Ask whether the agency one-year exception applies to you**, particularly if you came from the same industry.
- **Gather the evidence of your prior experience** in the same line of work.
- **Ask for a bank statement analysis alongside the agency analysis**, and compare the two qualifying incomes.
- **Get your CPA and your lender in one conversation before you file.**
- **Separate your banking** if you have not already.
- **Get fully underwritten rather than pre-qualified**, because on a file like yours a calculator number is meaningless.
