The short answer is generally no, and the exceptions are narrower than the internet implies.

Worth understanding properly, because this is a question where a borrower acting on a half-remembered rule can end up making an occupancy representation they cannot support, and that is not a paperwork problem.

## The premise

[[RULE:fha-occupancy-requirement]]

FHA insures owner-occupied housing. That is not a technicality attached to the program, it is the reason the program exists and the basis on which the insurance is priced. A borrower using FHA to assemble rental property is outside the purpose of the program.

So the default is one FHA loan at a time, on the home you occupy.

## The exceptions

[[RULE:fha-two-fha-loans]]

In general terms, the handbook contemplates specific situations rather than a general allowance. The recognized categories tend to involve:

**A relocation.** A borrower relocating for employment beyond a reasonable commuting distance from the existing FHA-financed home may be able to obtain a second FHA loan at the new location. Note the shape of this: it is about a genuine change in where you need to live, documented, not about wanting a second property.

**An increase in family size.** Where a family has outgrown the existing home and it no longer adequately houses them, with the equity position in the existing property also mattering.

**Vacating a jointly-owned property**, for example where a co-borrower leaves and will not return, such as after a divorce.

**A non-occupying co-borrower on the first loan** who is now buying their own primary residence. Someone who co-signed for a relative and is themselves a non-occupant is in a different position from an occupant.

Two things to know about all of them:

**They are documented, not asserted.** Expect to prove the relocation with employment documentation, or the family size change with specifics, or the vacating with a decree. Saying it is not establishing it.

**Equity position in the departing property frequently matters.** Several of these exceptions interact with how much equity you have in the home you are leaving.

## What people try instead, and the line they cross

Here is the part I want to be direct about.

The common idea is to buy a home with FHA, live in it briefly, move out, rent it, and buy another with FHA. If the intent at the time of the loan application was not to occupy the property as a primary residence, that is **occupancy fraud.** Not aggressive planning, not a gray area. A material misrepresentation on a federally insured mortgage application.

That is a different category of problem from a loan being declined, and it is worth saying plainly because plenty of content online treats it as a strategy.

What is legitimate: **circumstances genuinely change.** A borrower who bought intending to live there, lived there, and then relocated for work or outgrew the home is in an entirely different position from one who planned the sequence in advance. The first is what the exceptions exist for. The second is misrepresentation.

If you are reading this because you want to build a rental portfolio, FHA is the wrong tool and there are right ones.

## The right tools if you want investment property

**DSCR financing**, which qualifies on the property's rental income rather than your personal income, routinely vests in an LLC, and is purpose-built for exactly this. See [DSCR Loans for Scottsdale Investors](/blog/dscr-loans-scottsdale-investors/) and [Building a Rental Portfolio With DSCR](/blog/building-a-rental-portfolio-dscr/).

**Conventional investment property financing**, which is designed for non-owner-occupied purchases.

**Keeping the departing residence as a rental when you genuinely move**, which is a legitimate and common path with its own documentation requirements. See [Renting Out the House You Are Leaving](/blog/rental-income-departing-residence/).

**A second home, financed as a second home**, if that is what it is. Different pricing, different down payment, honest classification. See [Second Home vs Investment Property](/blog/scottsdale-second-home-vs-investment/).

## If you are moving and keeping the first house

This is the realistic version of the question for most people, and it is mostly about qualifying rather than about FHA eligibility.

The issue: **the payment on the home you are leaving counts in your debt-to-income ratio** unless it is sold and closed, or unless rental income is documented in a way your program accepts.

[[RULE:fha-dti-limits]]

Program treatment of a departing residence varies meaningfully between investors, which is one of the better arguments for a broker over a single lender on a file like this. Some will exclude the payment with an executed lease and a documented security deposit; some require the sale to have closed. Ask specifically. See [Renting Out the House You Are Leaving](/blog/rental-income-departing-residence/).

Also expect **reserve requirements to increase** when you own two properties, frequently measured as months of payments on both. This is the item that most often breaks these files.

If the sequencing is the problem rather than the qualifying, see [Buying Before You Sell](/blog/bridge-financing-buy-before-sell/).

## What to ask

- **Does one of the recognized exceptions actually fit my facts?** Be specific about your situation rather than asking generally.
- **What documentation would you need** to establish it?
- **How much equity do I need in the departing property**, if that applies?
- **How will you treat the departing payment** in my ratio, and would a lease change that?
- **What are my reserve requirements** with two properties?
- **Is FHA even the right program for my second purchase**, or would conventional serve me better?

That last question is the one worth asking honestly. If you are moving and keeping a rental, a conventional loan on the new primary and a DSCR refinance on the departing property is frequently a cleaner structure than trying to fit two FHA loans together.
