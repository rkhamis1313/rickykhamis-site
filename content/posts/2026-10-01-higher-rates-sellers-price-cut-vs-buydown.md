If you are selling right now and your agent is talking about a price reduction, stop and run one comparison first: **the same money, handed to the buyer as a rate concession instead of taken off your price, almost always buys a bigger payment reduction.**

Your buyer did not walk away because your house costs too much. They walked away because the payment stopped working. Those are different problems, and only one of them is solved by cutting your price.

## What actually changed

Rates moved, and they moved fast.

Freddie Mac's [Primary Mortgage Market Survey](https://www.freddiemac.com/pmms) put the 30-year fixed average at **7.03 percent on September 24, 2026**, up from 6.95 percent the week before. Daily trackers ran higher into the end of the month, hitting a one-year high and climbing roughly four tenths of a point in a single week.

The market responded immediately, and the data is unambiguous:

| Realtor.com, September 2026 | |
|---|---|
| Active listings with a price cut | **20.8%**, up 0.9 points year over year |
| How that ranks | Highest September share **since 2018**, highest any month since October 2022 |
| Active inventory | 1,161,615 homes, **up 5.4%** year over year |
| Homes under contract | **Down 4.1%** |

Read those last two lines together, because that is the whole story. **Inventory is up and pendings are down.** More homes are sitting, not because a wave of new sellers arrived, but because buyers stopped moving.

So one in five sellers reached for the price cut. Most of them reached for the wrong lever.

## Why the price cut is the inefficient tool

Here is the mechanic, and it is not complicated.

When you cut your price, the buyer's loan amount drops by the amount of the cut, less their down payment share. The payment falls by whatever that smaller balance produces at today's rate. That is it. The money works on the **balance**.

When you give that same money as a seller concession toward a rate buydown, it works on the **rate** instead. And because the rate is applied to the entire loan for the life of it, a dollar spent there moves the monthly payment substantially further than a dollar taken off the top.

That is the whole argument. You are not being generous. You are picking the lever with more leverage.

Three ways the concession can be deployed, and they are not equivalent:

**A permanent buydown.** Discount points paid at closing lower the note rate for the life of the loan. Best for a buyer who intends to stay, and the version that moves a qualifying calculation most durably.

**A temporary buydown**, often structured as 2-1 or 1-0. The rate is reduced in the first year or two, then steps to the note rate. Useful when a buyer expects income growth or expects to refinance, and it produces the largest first-year payment relief per dollar. Understand that it is temporary, and that the buyer must still qualify appropriately under the applicable guidelines.

**Closing cost assistance**, which helps a buyer who is cash-constrained rather than payment-constrained. Different problem, different tool. Diagnose before you prescribe.

**One hard caution.** Seller-paid concessions are capped, and the cap varies by loan type, by occupancy, and by the buyer's down payment. On some combinations the limit is tight enough that a large concession simply will not fit. **Find out what the buyer's specific loan permits before you offer anything**, because an offer that exceeds the cap gets restructured at the worst possible moment.

## The question to ask before you reduce

Ask your lender, not just your agent, to run this:

1. **What does a price reduction of X do to the buyer's monthly payment?**
2. **What does the same X, applied as a rate concession, do to the buyer's monthly payment?**
3. **What is the maximum concession the buyer's loan type actually allows?**
4. **Which one gets a borderline buyer across the qualifying line?**

That fourth question is the one that closes transactions. A buyer who is short on debt-to-income does not need a slightly smaller loan. **They need a lower payment**, and the concession route gets them there on fewer of your dollars.

I will run those four numbers for any agent or seller who asks, on the actual loan the buyer is using. It takes about ten minutes and it is frequently the difference between a contract and another thirty days on market.

## What else to do, in order

**Get your own home underwritten before you list, if you are buying next.** In a market where pendings are down and inventory is up, a seller who is also a buyer has real leverage on the purchase side. That leverage evaporates if your offer is contingent on a sale you have not structured. See [Moving to Scottsdale from California](/blog/moving-to-scottsdale-from-california/) for how departing residence treatment works, which applies equally to a local move.

**Price correctly from day one rather than chasing.** The data above shows what chasing looks like: one in five listings already cutting. The listings taking cuts are mostly the ones that launched above the market and are now following it down. Launching correctly and holding is a stronger position than launching high and reducing twice.

**Make the house easy to finance.** Deferred roof, an unpermitted addition, a condo project with thin reserves, or an association in litigation can all narrow the pool of buyers who can get a loan on your property at all. In Scottsdale, condominium project issues quietly disqualify more buyers than price does. See [Scottsdale Luxury Condo Financing](/blog/scottsdale-luxury-condo-project-approval/).

**Know who your buyer actually is.** Self-employed, equity compensated, retired on distributions, relocating from out of state. Each qualifies differently, and a listing that attracts a buyer your market's lenders handle badly will sit. That is most of what I write about, and it is why I pay attention to which buyer a given property attracts.

## The honest version

Rates at a one-year high are not good news for sellers, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. Demand has cooled, inventory has risen, and the balance has shifted toward buyers.

But the reflexive response, cut the price, is the most expensive way to solve a problem that is usually about the payment. Before you take real money off your bottom line, spend ten minutes finding out what the same money does when it is pointed at the rate instead.

Most sellers never ask. That is the opportunity.
