A homeowner with a rate they like and a payment they would like smaller has two tools, and most only hear about one of them.

## What each one does

**A refinance** replaces your existing loan with a new one. New rate, new term, new closing costs, full underwriting. Your payment changes because the loan changed.

**A recast** keeps your existing loan entirely. Same rate, same term, same note. You apply a lump sum to principal and the servicer **re-amortizes** the remaining balance over the remaining term, which produces a lower payment. There is usually a modest administrative fee and generally no underwriting at all.

That distinction is the whole point: a recast lowers your payment **without giving up your rate.**

## Why the recast is frequently the right answer right now

Consider a homeowner who bought when rates were materially lower than today. They have a rate that is, in current terms, an asset. They have come into some money, from a bonus, an inheritance, the sale of another property, or a liquidity event.

**If they refinance**, they lower the balance and lose the rate. Almost always a bad trade in this environment.

**If they just pay the lump sum toward principal**, the balance drops, the loan pays off sooner, and the **payment does not change.** Most people do not realize this. Prepaying principal shortens the loan; it does not reduce the required monthly payment.

**If they recast**, the balance drops and the payment is recalculated downward over the remaining term. Same rate, lower payment, modest fee, no underwriting.

For that homeowner the recast wins on nearly every dimension. It is also the option that requires somebody to tell them it exists, which is why it goes unused.

## When a recast is the better choice

- **Your current rate is better than today's market.** Decisive. Do not refinance away a good rate to use cash you could deploy with a recast instead.
- **You have a lump sum** and want monthly relief rather than a shorter term.
- **Your income or credit has changed** in a way that would make a refinance harder. A recast generally involves no requalification.
- **You want to avoid closing costs**, which a recast largely does.
- **You just closed on a new house** and your old one sold afterward. This is the classic case and it is extremely common among move-up buyers. See [Buying Before You Sell](/blog/bridge-financing-buy-before-sell/).

That last scenario deserves emphasis. A buyer who purchases with a larger loan because their equity was tied up, then receives the proceeds when the old house sells, is in exactly the position a recast was designed for. **Ask about recast eligibility before you close the purchase**, because loan programs and servicers differ on whether they permit it.

## When a refinance is the better choice

- **Today's rate is better than yours.** Then the rate itself is the prize.
- **You need cash out.** A recast puts money in; it cannot take money out.
- **You want to remove mortgage insurance** and a recast will not accomplish it under your loan's terms.
- **You want a different term or product.** Recasting cannot change thirty years to fifteen, or move you off an adjustable rate.
- **You want to remove someone from the loan**, which requires a new loan. See [Divorce and the Mortgage in Arizona](/blog/divorce-mortgage-arizona/).
- **Your loan does not permit recasting.** Which is the most common reason people cannot use it.

## The limitations nobody mentions until you ask

**Not every loan can be recast.** Eligibility depends on the loan type and the servicer's policy. Government loans are commonly excluded. Some portfolio and non-QM loans do not permit it. Ask your servicer directly, by name, about your specific loan.

**There is usually a minimum lump sum**, either a dollar figure or a percentage of the balance.

**There may be a seasoning requirement**, a minimum number of payments made before a recast is allowed.

**The servicer, not the originating lender, controls it.** Loans get sold and serviced elsewhere, so the policy that governs is the current servicer's. If a recast is part of your plan, confirm it with whoever is servicing the loan today, and understand that servicing can transfer.

**It does not change your rate or your term.** The payoff date stays the same. You are spreading a smaller balance over the same remaining years.

## The arithmetic to run before you do either

Ask your lender or servicer for these numbers side by side:

1. Your current balance, rate, remaining term and payment.
2. The payment after a recast with your specific lump sum, plus the recast fee.
3. The payment on a refinance at today's rate for the same remaining term, with all closing costs.
4. The payment if you simply apply the lump sum as a principal prepayment and recast nothing, which is the same payment you have now, with an earlier payoff.
5. The total interest paid over your realistic holding period under each.

That last line matters because the answer depends on how long you actually keep the loan. A refinance with meaningful closing costs needs time to earn them back.

And one more consideration that is not on the sheet: **is using the lump sum on the house the best use of it at all?** Reserves, a business opportunity, a retirement contribution, or paying off higher-rate debt may beat reducing a low-rate mortgage. That is a conversation for your financial advisor, and it deserves to happen before you commit the money.

## The one-line version

If your rate is better than today's market and you have cash you want to convert into a lower payment, ask your servicer whether your loan can be recast. It is the cheapest payment reduction available to most homeowners in this market, and it is the one almost nobody is told about.
