This is the most consequential thing about an FHA loan that borrowers understand least at closing, and it is the factor that most often decides whether FHA was the right choice in hindsight.

## The two premiums

FHA mortgage insurance comes in two parts.

**An upfront premium**, charged at closing, commonly financed into the loan balance rather than paid in cash. Note what that means: it increases what you owe from day one, so you begin slightly behind on equity.

**An annual premium**, collected monthly as part of your payment. This is the one that matters over time.

## How long the annual premium lasts

[[RULE:fha-mip-duration]]

The key distinction, and the one to get answered about your specific loan: **depending on the terms, the annual premium can run for a very long period or for the full term of the loan.** It is not automatically tied to reaching an equity threshold the way conventional private mortgage insurance is.

Ask your lender this exact question before you close:

> *"On this specific loan, does the annual mortgage insurance premium ever terminate, and if so, when and on what condition?"*

Get the answer in writing. It is the difference between a cost that ends and a cost that does not, and over a long hold that is a lot of money.

## Why this is different from conventional PMI

Conventional private mortgage insurance is generally **cancellable**. As the loan pays down and the property appreciates, PMI can come off and the loan continues without it. There are rules about when and how, and they are worth knowing, but the premise is that it ends.

FHA's annual premium may not work that way. That is the central trade in choosing FHA: **the program gave you access on terms conventional would not have, and you may pay for that access for a long time.**

For a borrower who genuinely needed FHA to buy at all, that is a reasonable trade and it beat not owning. For a borrower who could have qualified conventionally and chose FHA because the monthly payment looked slightly better at the outset, it is frequently a bad trade that compounds for years.

That comparison belongs in your decision before you close, not after. See [FHA or Conventional](/blog/fha-vs-conventional-arizona/).

## The exits

**Refinance into a conventional loan.** The main one, and for many FHA borrowers the actual plan.

The mechanics: once you have enough equity, you refinance into a conventional loan with no mortgage insurance, or with cancellable PMI. The equity can come from paying down the balance, from appreciation, or from both.

Three honest cautions:

- **It depends on rates at the time.** If rates are higher when you have the equity, the refinance can cost more in interest than it saves in insurance. Run the comparison; do not assume.
- **It depends on an appraisal.** You need the value to support it.
- **It is a full new loan.** New closing costs, new underwriting, new qualification. If your income or credit has deteriorated, it may not be available when you want it.

Which is why **a refinance is an option, never a plan.** Any structure that only works if you refinance is a structure that has not accounted for rates staying where they are.

**Sell the property.** Pays off the loan and ends the insurance.

**An FHA streamline refinance**, which is worth understanding for what it does and does not do.

[[RULE:fha-streamline-refinance]]

A streamline refinance stays within FHA, which means the mortgage insurance structure generally continues. It is a tool for lowering a rate with reduced documentation, not a tool for escaping mortgage insurance. People conflate these constantly.

**Pay the loan off.** Technically an exit, rarely the plan.

## What to do with this

**If you are about to take an FHA loan:**

Ask the termination question above and get it in writing. Then ask your lender to show you the total cost of FHA versus conventional over the period you realistically expect to keep the loan, with the insurance line broken out separately. Not over thirty years, over your actual horizon.

If the numbers are close and the difference is your credit score, that is worth sixty to ninety days of credit work before you shop. Moving from the FHA case into the conventional case is worth more than almost anything else available to you. See [Fix the Credit First](/blog/credit-repair-before-mortgage/).

**If you already have an FHA loan:**

Find out where you actually stand. Your current balance, a realistic current value, and therefore your equity position. Then ask whether a conventional refinance makes sense **at today's rates**, with all costs included, against what you are paying in insurance.

Sometimes the answer is clearly yes and nobody has told you. Sometimes it is clearly no because the rate difference swamps the insurance saving. Either way it is a ten-minute calculation and it is worth doing annually rather than assuming.

One more option worth knowing about if your rate is already good and you have come into money: a **recast** lowers your payment by applying a lump sum and re-amortizing at your existing rate, without giving up that rate. It does not remove FHA mortgage insurance, but if your goal is a lower payment rather than escaping the insurance, it may be the cheaper move. See [Recast vs Refinance](/blog/recast-vs-refinance/).

## The short version

Find out, specifically, whether your FHA annual premium ever ends. If it does not, the refinance out is the real plan and it depends on rates and an appraisal you do not control. Price that honestly before you close, and if credit is the only thing standing between you and conventional, fix the credit first.
