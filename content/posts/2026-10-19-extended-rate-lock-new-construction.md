You signed a purchase agreement on a home that delivers next summer. The rate you were quoted is for a thirty day lock. Those two facts do not fit together, and reconciling them is one of the more consequential decisions in a new construction purchase.

## The basic problem

A rate lock is a lender's commitment to honor a price for a defined period. The lender manages the risk of that commitment, and the longer the commitment, the more risk, so the more it costs. Always. There is no free long lock.

On a resale with a thirty day escrow, this barely matters. On new construction with a delivery window measured in months, and a delivery date that moves because of weather, inspections, supply and trade scheduling, it matters a great deal.

You have three structural choices.

## Choice one: float, and lock later

Do not lock now. Lock when delivery is close enough that a standard lock period covers it.

**What you are buying:** nothing, which means you pay nothing extra now. If rates fall between now and then, you capture the improvement entirely.

**What you are risking:** everything. If rates rise, you close at the higher rate, and your qualifying is retested against the higher payment. That second part is the one that hurts. A buyer who qualified comfortably at one rate can fail at a rate materially higher, and now they are closing on a house they contracted for and cannot finance. That happens, and it is a very bad week.

**When floating is defensible:** you have substantial room in your debt-to-income ratio, meaningful reserves, and the capacity to absorb a higher payment without the approval breaking. Essentially, when you can afford to be wrong.

## Choice two: an extended lock

A lock written for a long period, commonly in increments of several months out to a year or more on programs that offer it.

**What it costs:** a price adjustment, charged as points or a higher rate, scaling with duration. Some lenders charge an upfront fee, sometimes refundable at closing, sometimes not.

**What it buys:** certainty. Your rate is known, your payment is known, and your approval does not get retested against a market that moved.

**The question to ask that most buyers do not:** does the extended lock include a **float-down**? A float-down lets you take a lower rate if the market improves during the lock period, usually once, usually subject to conditions and a margin. Without one, you are protected against rates rising and locked out of rates falling, which in a market where many expect rates to decline is a real cost.

Float-down terms vary enormously between lenders, and this is one of the better reasons to have a broker shopping multiple investors rather than accepting one shelf.

## Choice three: a short lock, extended as needed

Lock conventionally and pay extension fees as the delivery date slips.

**Sometimes this is cheapest** if delivery is genuinely near and only slips a little.

**Frequently it is a trap**, because extension pricing is charged at the time of extension, in market conditions you do not control. A lock extended three times in a rising market can cost more than the extended lock would have, with none of the certainty.

## The questions to ask before you choose

**What is the realistic delivery date, not the contract date?** Ask the builder what the current average is on homes at your stage in this community. Then add time. Delivery dates in this market have been optimistic more often than pessimistic.

**What does the extended lock cost, in points, for each available duration?** Get it in writing.

**Is there a float-down, and on what terms?** How much must the market improve, how many times can you use it, is there a fee, and what is the deadline.

**What happens if delivery slips past the lock expiration?** Get the extension cost schedule before you commit, not when you need it.

**Is the lock transferable if the loan program changes?** Occasionally a file moves programs mid-process. Ask.

**What happens if the appraisal comes in low?** On new construction the appraisal is ordered late, sometimes close to delivery, and a value below contract price is a live possibility. See [Appraisal Gaps on an Arizona Contract](/blog/appraisal-gap-arizona-contract/).

**Will I be requalified before closing?** Generally yes, and income, employment and credit get re-verified. Which means a job change, a new car loan or a new credit card during the build is a genuine risk to the closing, not a technicality.

## How this interacts with the builder's incentive

If the builder's incentive is tied to their lender, the comparison between their offer and an outside offer has to be run on the **same lock period.** A thirty day quote compared against a nine month extended lock is not a comparison, and it is the most common way a builder lender quote looks better than it is.

Ask both lenders to quote the lock duration you actually need. See [Builder's Lender or Your Own](/blog/builder-lender-vs-your-own-lender/).

## The decision framework

**Tight on ratios, limited reserves:** buy the extended lock. The certainty is worth more than the cost, because the downside case is not a worse payment, it is no loan.

**Comfortable on ratios, strong reserves, long delivery:** floating is defensible, and an extended lock with a float-down is the middle path worth pricing.

**Delivery inside sixty days:** a standard or modestly extended lock is usually right.

**Any case:** ask about the float-down. It is the single most undershopped term in new construction financing.

## During the build, do not disturb your file

- No job changes if you can avoid them, and tell your lender immediately if one is coming.
- No new debt. Not a car, not furniture on credit, not a new card.
- No large unexplained deposits. See [Large Deposits and NSF Items](/blog/large-deposits-nsf-bank-statements/).
- Keep reserves intact. Design center upgrades paid in cash can quietly destroy the reserve position your approval depends on.
