Here is a way to lose a closing in week three: negotiate a generous concession, structure a buydown with it, cover the buyer's title and escrow charges on top, and then discover at underwriting that the total exceeds what the loan allows. Something has to come back out, somebody is unhappy, and occasionally the deal does not survive the conversation.

This is avoidable with one phone call before the contract is signed.

## What a concession cap is

Loan programs limit how much an interested party can contribute toward a buyer's costs. The limit exists so a seller cannot inflate the price and hand the difference back as a credit, which would distort the collateral value the loan is secured by.

The cap is expressed as a percentage of the sale price or appraised value, and it varies by:

- **Loan type.** Conventional, FHA, VA and non-QM programs all treat it differently.
- **Occupancy.** Owner occupied generally gets the most room. Second homes less. Investment property least.
- **Loan-to-value.** This is the one people forget. On conventional financing the cap tightens considerably as the down payment shrinks, so the buyer putting the least down, who needs the most help, frequently has the least room to receive it.

The specific percentages change over time and by investor overlay. I am deliberately not publishing numbers here, because a number in a blog post is how somebody ends up relying on a stale figure in a live contract. Get the current cap confirmed against the actual loan.

## What counts against the cap

This is where files go wrong. The cap is not a separate allowance for the buydown. It is one bucket, and most seller-paid items go in it:

- Discount points, whether funding a temporary buydown or a permanent rate reduction
- Origination and lender fees paid on the buyer's behalf
- Title, escrow and settlement charges
- Recording fees and transfer charges
- Prepaid interest, and frequently prepaid taxes and insurance
- Homeowner association transfer fees and capitalization charges, depending on program treatment
- The buyer's home warranty, in some program treatments

A seller who agrees to a 3-2-1 buydown and then agrees to pay closing costs has often agreed to two things that together exceed one limit.

Separately, some items are treated differently or not counted at all, and the treatment varies by program. Repairs required by the appraiser are generally handled as repairs rather than concessions. Items a seller customarily pays in the local market may be treated differently from items the buyer customarily pays. Do not reason from first principles here. Ask.

## The sequence that prevents the problem

**1. Identify the buyer's loan type and occupancy** as early in the negotiation as you can. The pre-approval letter should say.

**2. Call the lender and ask for the cap in writing** for that specific loan, occupancy and down payment.

**3. Price the buydown** you want to fund. See [The 2-1](/blog/how-a-2-1-buydown-actually-works/) and [the 3-2-1](/blog/3-2-1-buydown-explained/).

**4. Add every other seller-paid item.** All of them.

**5. Compare the total to the cap.** If it fits, write it. If it does not, decide what to cut before the contract rather than after.

**6. Write the concession so it can flex.** Language that caps the seller's contribution at a dollar amount "or the maximum allowed by the buyer's loan program, whichever is less" prevents a renegotiation. Have your broker and the appropriate professionals confirm the wording you use; contract language is their call, not mine.

## For listing agents specifically

Three practical habits:

**Ask about the cap when you take the listing**, not when the offer arrives. If you know the structure you intend to market, you know the contribution it requires, and you can tell the seller which buyer types it works for.

**Treat a low-down-payment offer differently.** That buyer may want the most help and have the least room to receive it. Know that before you counter.

**Investor and second home offers have tighter room.** A concession strategy built for owner occupants may not transfer. See [Second Home vs Investment Property](/blog/scottsdale-second-home-vs-investment/).

## For buyers

If you are negotiating for help, understand that your own financing structure determines how much help you are permitted to accept. A larger down payment can increase the concession room available to you, which occasionally means a buyer is better off putting more down and taking more help than putting less down and being capped out of it.

That is a real calculation with a real answer, and it depends on your numbers.
