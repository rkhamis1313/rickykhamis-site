You are buying a new house and keeping the old one as a rental. Reasonable plan, particularly if your current rate is low enough that selling feels like throwing something away.

Whether it works depends on one question: will the underwriter let the rental income offset the payment, or will you have to carry both payments out of your own income?

The answer varies more by program than almost anything else in lending, which is worth knowing before you commit to a plan.

## Why the departing residence is the problem

An underwriter counts your obligations. The payment on a house you still own is an obligation, so by default it goes into your debt-to-income ratio alongside the payment on the new house.

For most move-up buyers, carrying both payments out of earned income does not work. So the question becomes whether the rental income can offset the departing payment, and the answer depends on documentation.

## What generally lets the income count

Requirements vary by program and investor, so confirm against yours. Broadly, the stronger your documentation the better your treatment:

**An executed lease agreement.** A signed lease with a real tenant is the baseline evidence.

**Documented receipt of the security deposit and frequently the first month's rent.** Deposited and showing in your bank statements. A lease with no money received is a plan, not income.

**A market rent determination from an appraiser.** On some programs an appraiser's opinion of market rent, generally on Form 1007, supports or substitutes for a lease.

**A history of rental income on your tax returns**, which is the strongest evidence and which by definition you do not have on a property you have just started renting.

That last point is the structural issue: **a first-time landlord has the weakest documentation case**, precisely when they most need the income to count. Programs differ substantially in how they handle that, which is the central reason to have a broker checking several rather than accepting the first answer.

## The haircut

Rental income is almost never counted at face value. Programs apply a vacancy and maintenance factor, commonly in the range of a quarter of gross rent, so a meaningful share of the rent does not count toward your ratio.

Then the net is compared to the full payment on the departing residence including principal, interest, taxes, insurance and association dues. If rent net of the factor exceeds that payment, the surplus may help you. If it falls short, the shortfall is a liability in your ratio.

Run that arithmetic honestly before you build a plan on it. A property with a low rate and high association dues frequently nets less than people expect.

## The equity test people do not know about

Some programs require you to demonstrate a minimum level of equity in the departing residence before they will allow rental income to be used at all. The logic is that a landlord with little equity is more likely to walk away if the rental goes badly.

Where that requirement applies, it generally needs an appraisal or an acceptable valuation of the departing property, which is a cost and a step people do not plan for.

Ask about this specifically. It is a common surprise and it is entirely knowable in advance.

## Reserves get harder with two properties

Expect reserve requirements to increase, frequently measured as months of payments on **both** properties, and on some programs scaling further with the number of financed properties you own.

This is the item that most often breaks these files. A buyer who puts everything into the new down payment and plans to rent the old house frequently fails the reserve test. Reserves are tested after closing and they are not negotiable on most programs.

## Two things to get right that have nothing to do with the loan

**The association's rules.** Many Scottsdale communities restrict rentals, through minimum lease terms, caps on the number of rented units, waiting periods after purchase, or outright prohibitions on short term rentals. The association's documents govern regardless of what the city permits. Read them before you plan on rental income. See [When HOA Documents Delay a Closing](/blog/hoa-document-delays-closing/).

**Short term rental is a different proposition entirely.** Arizona cities operate within a state framework but have licensing and tax registration requirements, and association restrictions frequently bind more tightly than municipal rules. For underwriting purposes, short term rental income is treated differently and not every program will use it. See [Scottsdale Short-Term Rental Income Loans](/blog/scottsdale-short-term-rental-income-loan/) and [Short-Term Rental DSCR](/blog/short-term-rental-dscr-scottsdale/).

## When the departing residence approach does not work

**DSCR on the new purchase is the wrong tool** if the new house is your primary residence, since DSCR is investment property financing. But DSCR is very much the right tool if you are refinancing the departing residence into investor financing, because it qualifies on that property's rent rather than your income. That can be a clean way to separate the two properties financially. See [DSCR Loans for Scottsdale Investors](/blog/dscr-loans-scottsdale-investors/).

**Asset depletion on the new purchase**, deriving income from your liquid portfolio, sidesteps the ratio problem entirely for an asset-rich buyer. See [Asset Depletion and Asset Qualifier Loans](/blog/asset-depletion-asset-qualifier-loans/).

**No-ratio financing** applies no debt-to-income test at all, which makes the departing payment a non-issue. Credit, assets, reserves and the property carry it.

**Selling, honestly.** Sometimes the right answer. Run the numbers on holding a property at a thin margin with real vacancy and maintenance risk before deciding that a low rate makes it automatic.

## And the thing you must not do

Do not tell a lender you are renting the departing residence and then not rent it, or tell them you are selling and then keep it. Occupancy and income representations are material and misrepresenting them is loan fraud, not an administrative detail.

## Before you commit to the plan

- **Get the ratio run both ways**, with and without rental income counted.
- **Ask what documentation your program requires** for the income to count.
- **Ask whether an equity test applies** to the departing residence.
- **Get a realistic rent estimate** from someone who manages property in the area.
- **Apply the vacancy factor yourself** before you believe the number.
- **Confirm reserve requirements for both properties.**
- **Read the association's rental restrictions.**
- **Get the lease executed and the deposit documented** before you need it in underwriting.
