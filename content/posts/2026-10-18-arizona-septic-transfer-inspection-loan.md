If you are buying a property on a septic system in Arizona, there is a required step most buyers have never heard of and most out-of-state buyers find out about in week three. It is not complicated. It just takes time that a thirty day closing does not have spare.

## What the requirement is

Arizona requires that an on-site wastewater treatment facility, which is what a septic system is in regulatory language, be **inspected before the property transfers ownership**, with the inspection performed by a qualified inspector, a report completed, and the discharge authorization transferred to the new owner. The Arizona Department of Environmental Quality administers the framework, and county environmental health departments including Maricopa County administer it locally.

Three practical facts follow from that:

**It is not optional and it is not the same as a home inspection.** A general home inspector looking at the house is not the septic transfer inspection. It is a separate, specific inspection by someone qualified to do it.

**It has a time window.** The inspection has to be completed within a defined period before transfer, and the transfer paperwork has to be submitted within a defined period after. Your escrow officer and your agent will know the current windows; confirm them rather than assuming.

**Somebody has to schedule it.** This is where files die. Everyone assumes somebody else is handling it.

## Why it matters to the loan

A lender's interest is that the property be safe, sound and habitable, and functioning wastewater disposal is squarely inside that. Two ways it touches the loan:

**The appraiser notes the system** and may condition the appraisal on documentation that it is functional.

**A failed inspection becomes a repair condition.** If the inspection finds the system failing, the lender generally will not fund until it is repaired or replaced. Septic repairs range from modest to very expensive, and a full replacement on a difficult parcel is a serious number. That negotiation, who pays and on what timeline, happens in the middle of your escrow with a closing date running.

## Where files actually get into trouble

**Nobody scheduled it.** Week three arrives, the inspection has not been ordered, qualified inspectors in the north valley have a queue, and the closing date moves.

**The pumping requirement.** Inspection typically requires the tank to be pumped and opened for examination. That is a separate vendor and a separate appointment, and scheduling two vendors in sequence takes longer than scheduling one.

**The system cannot be located.** On an older parcel with no records, locating the tank and the leach field is occasionally an exercise in archaeology. Add days.

**The inspection finds a problem.** Now you have a repair negotiation, a bid process, a permit possibly, and a lender waiting. This is the scenario to protect against with calendar room.

**Permit records do not match the house.** A system permitted for a three bedroom house serving a home that has since had two bedrooms added is a finding. See the permitted-additions problem generally: unpermitted work on an older property creates several separate issues at once.

**Winter and holiday scheduling.** Vendor availability in the north valley is not uniform across the year.

## What to do, in order

**1. Ask whether the property is on septic before you write.** Not during escrow. If the listing does not say, ask the listing agent directly. Properties in Cave Creek, Carefree, Rio Verde, north Scottsdale and unincorporated county land frequently are.

**2. Put it in the contract and name who schedules it.** Arizona practice varies by transaction and it is a negotiable item. What matters is that the contract is specific, so nobody assumes. Your broker advises you on contract language; that is their call, not your lender's.

**3. Schedule it in week one.** The single most useful thing in this post. Not when the inspection period is closing. Week one.

**4. Schedule the pump-out and the inspection together** if the inspector does not handle both.

**5. Build calendar room into the closing date.** If you are writing on a septic property, a thirty day close is tight and a thirty five to forty day close is realistic. Negotiate the date accordingly rather than hoping.

**6. Get the permit records** and compare them to the house as it exists, particularly the bedroom count.

**7. Decide in advance who pays for repairs** if the inspection fails, and at what threshold you walk. Having that conversation before you need it is much better than having it under time pressure.

## If you are on septic you are probably also on a well

The two travel together on desert acreage, and the well has its own documentation requirements including potability testing and, for shared wells, a recorded agreement. Both items run in parallel and both add days. See [Well Water and Mortgage Financing in Arizona](/blog/well-water-mortgage-arizona/).

Minimum separation distances between the well and the septic system are also a requirement, which is one reason the two get examined together.

## The one-sentence version

If there is a septic system, schedule the transfer inspection in week one and negotiate a closing date with room in it, because this is the item that most reliably costs people their closing date on desert property.
