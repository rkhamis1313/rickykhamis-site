Every other loan program asks one question about your money: what percentage of your income goes to debt?

VA asks a better one. **After everything is paid, how much is actually left to live on, and is that enough for a household your size?**

That second test is called residual income, no other program uses it, and it is the reason VA files get approved that would be declined anywhere else.

## How it works

[[RULE:va-residual-income]]

The calculation takes your qualifying income and subtracts not only the mortgage payment and your debts, but also estimated taxes, and estimated utilities and maintenance on the home. What remains is your residual income.

That figure is then compared against a requirement that scales with two things most programs ignore entirely:

**Your family size.** A household of two and a household of six do not need the same amount left over, and VA is the only program that says so in its underwriting.

**Your region of the country.** The requirement differs geographically, which is a more honest reflection of reality than a single national ratio.

## Why this matters so much

A conventional or FHA file is largely decided by a ratio. If your debts exceed a share of your income, you are over the limit, regardless of whether what remains would comfortably support your household.

VA will look at a veteran who is over on ratio and ask whether there is nevertheless plenty left each month. Frequently there is, and the loan is approved.

[[RULE:va-dti-compensating]]

The practical effect: **the ratio is a guideline and residual income is the test.** A strong residual position can carry a file past a ratio that would end it elsewhere.

That is why the sentence "your debt-to-income is too high" is a weaker objection on a VA file than on any other, and why it is worth asking your lender directly what your residual income figure is rather than accepting a ratio-based no.

## Where it works in your favor

**Larger households with solid income.** The requirement scales up with family size, but so does the recognition that you are housing more people.

**Veterans with rental income.** If you are buying multi-unit and the other units produce income, that strengthens the residual picture considerably. See [The Fourplex Play](/blog/va-fourplex-house-hacking/).

[[RULE:va-rental-income-multiunit]]

**No monthly mortgage insurance.** This is quietly one of the biggest residual income advantages in the program. The money a comparable FHA borrower spends on a premium every month is money that stays in a VA borrower's residual calculation.

[[RULE:va-no-mortgage-insurance]]

**Non-taxable income.** Disability compensation and certain other income is generally non-taxable, and non-taxable income can be grossed up on many programs, which improves both your ratio and your residual figure. Ask your lender specifically whether they are doing this, because it is frequently missed.

## Where it works against you

**Household size counts both ways.** A larger family needs a larger residual figure, and dependents are counted.

**The utilities and maintenance estimate is included** whether or not you think it is realistic for your property.

**Consumer debt hurts twice.** A car payment reduces your residual directly and raises your ratio at the same time. Clearing a payment before you apply helps more on a VA file than almost anywhere else.

## What to ask your lender

> *"What is my residual income figure, what is the requirement for my family size and region, and how much room do I have?"*

A competent VA originator answers that in one go. If they cannot, or if they have only quoted you a debt-to-income ratio, they are evaluating your file the way a conventional lender would and you are not getting the benefit of the program.

That question alone will tell you whether the person you are talking to has actually closed VA loans or is treating yours as a conventional file with a different cover sheet.
