Of all the things that push a closing date, association paperwork is the most frustrating, because nobody in the transaction controls it and everybody in the transaction depends on it.

In Scottsdale this is not a minor item. Plenty of addresses sit inside a master association plus a village or sub-association, and some add a club obligation on top. Each entity has its own documents, its own fees, its own processing time, and its own idea of urgency.

## What actually has to be produced

**The association demand or estoppel statement.** The association's statement of what is owed on the property: current dues status, any unpaid balance, pending special assessments, transfer and capitalization fees, and the amount required at closing. Escrow cannot close without it, and the association issues it on its own schedule.

**The governing documents.** CC&Rs, bylaws, articles, rules and regulations. The buyer needs them to know what they are agreeing to, and the lender needs certain things from them.

**The financial statements and budget.** Required for condominium project review and increasingly scrutinized on other attached housing. This is where reserve adequacy gets evaluated.

**Insurance evidence.** The master policy, with limits and deductibles, and in some cases fidelity coverage. On a condominium the lender has specific requirements and a deficient master policy is a real, and fixable, problem that requires the association to act.

**Litigation disclosure.** Whether the association is involved in litigation, and what kind. Construction defect litigation can stop financing on a project outright.

## Why this breaks closings

**Associations are not oriented to your deadline.** Many are managed by a company handling hundreds of communities. Document requests go into a queue. Some charge rush fees to move faster, which is worth paying.

**Self-managed associations are slower and less predictable.** A volunteer treasurer who travels can hold up a closing for a week without any awareness that they are doing it.

**Multiple associations multiply everything.** Two associations is two queues running in parallel at different speeds. You close at the speed of the slowest.

**The documents reveal a problem.** A pending special assessment, insufficient insurance, a litigation disclosure, or an owner-occupancy ratio that fails project review. Now you have a real issue rather than a scheduling one, and you want the maximum possible runway to deal with it.

**Fees were not budgeted.** Transfer fees, capitalization or working capital contributions, document fees, and rush fees. They are real and on some communities they are substantial. Who pays them is a contract matter.

## The pending special assessment problem

Worth its own section because it affects two things at once.

A pending or recently approved special assessment is a disclosed obligation. It can:

**Change your qualifying ratio**, if it is payable in installments, because it becomes a monthly obligation in your debt-to-income calculation. A buyer who was comfortable can become tight.

**Require payoff at closing**, which is a cash item nobody planned for.

**Signal deferred maintenance**, which on a condominium is exactly what project review is looking for.

Ask specifically: is any special assessment pending, proposed, or under discussion? The last one does not appear on a demand statement and an association board will usually answer honestly if asked directly.

## The week one checklist

**Order the documents the day escrow opens.** Not after inspection. Day one.

**Identify every association that bills the address.** Ask the listing agent, then verify. Assuming there is one when there are two is a common and expensive mistake.

**Pay the rush fee** if one is available. It is the cheapest schedule insurance in the transaction.

**Ask the specific questions** rather than waiting for documents to answer them:
- What are the total monthly dues, for every entity?
- Is any special assessment pending, approved or under discussion?
- Is the association involved in any litigation?
- For a condominium: what is the owner occupancy ratio, and what share of units does any single owner hold?
- Is any club membership mandatory?

**Get the complete monthly obligation in writing** for your lender. Every dollar goes into your ratio, and on a file where income was already rebuilt downward, this number can decide the approval. See [HOA and Club Dues in Your Ratio](/blog/scottsdale-hoa-club-dues-debt-to-income/).

**On a condominium, start project review immediately.** The association documents feed it, and the project can disqualify the property regardless of how strong you are. See [Condo and Project Approval](/blog/scottsdale-luxury-condo-project-approval/) and [Non-Warrantable Condo Financing](/blog/non-warrantable-condo-financing-scottsdale/).

## Negotiate the closing date accordingly

A property with one well-managed association adds little. A property with a master association, a sub-association, and a club obligation deserves extra time in the contract rather than optimism.

This is the same principle as everywhere else in a transaction: a forty day close that holds beats a thirty day close that slips. See [A Realistic Escrow Timeline](/blog/escrow-timeline-arizona-30-days/).

## For listing agents

You can eliminate most of this as a problem on your own listings.

**Order the documents when you take the listing.** Have them ready to hand to a buyer's agent with the offer. It shortens escrow, it signals competence, and it surfaces any problem while you still have time to address it rather than during somebody's escrow.

**Know the exact dues figure** for every entity billing your listing, and know whether any club membership is mandatory. Buyers' lenders will ask and a wrong answer costs a week.

**Find out about pending assessments before you list.** If there is one, you want to disclose it from a position of preparation.
