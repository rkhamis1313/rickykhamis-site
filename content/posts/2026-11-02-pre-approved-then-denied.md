You had a letter. You wrote an offer on the strength of it. You are now weeks in, your earnest money is at risk, and the answer has changed to no.

This is the worst version of a mortgage decline because it happens after you have committed. It is also, more often than people realize, preventable.

## The thing nobody told you about the letter

A pre-approval is not a loan commitment. It is a conditional opinion based on what was reviewed at the time, and the conditions include the property, the appraisal, the title, and a re-verification of you before closing.

Worse, the word itself is unreliable. At some lenders a pre-approval means documents were collected and read by a human. At others it means someone typed your stated income into a calculator and printed letterhead. Those two letters look identical and are not remotely the same thing.

So the first question is not why it fell apart. It is **what was actually done before that letter was issued.** See [Pre-Qualified, Pre-Approved, Underwritten](/blog/underwritten-preapproval-vs-prequalification/).

## What changes between the letter and the closing

Six things, roughly in order of how often they are the culprit.

**1. A real underwriter finally read the file.** If the letter came from a calculator, this is the moment the actual analysis happens. For a self-employed borrower this is where the qualifying income gets rebuilt from the returns and comes out well below what the business feels like it earns. The decline was baked in from the beginning; it just surfaced late.

**2. The property entered the picture.** The letter was about you. The loan is about you **and** the house. The appraisal, the condo project, the condition items, the water and septic documentation, the title work. None of that existed when the letter was written and all of it can stop the loan. See [Why Won't They Lend](/blog/why-mortgage-denied/).

**3. Your credit got re-pulled.** It generally is, before closing. A new account, a higher balance, a late payment, or even a cluster of inquiries can change your score, your ratio, or your pricing tier, and any of those can break the approval.

**4. Your employment got re-verified.** Also standard. A job change, a reduction in hours, a switch from salary to commission, or a move to self-employment between the letter and the closing is a serious problem.

**5. Your assets got re-examined.** Reserves are tested after closing. Money spent between the letter and the closing is money the file was counting on.

**6. Something was disclosed late.** A support obligation, a co-signed loan, a judgment, a past property. An underwriter finds these. A borrower who did not mention one now has a credibility problem on top of whatever the item itself causes.

## The things you can actually control

Treat your file as frozen from application to closing. This list is short and it is not optional.

**Do not open new credit.** Not a car, not furniture, not a store card at a register for a discount, not a credit line someone offers you at closing on something else. This is the single most common self-inflicted decline in the business.

**Do not change jobs if you can avoid it.** If a change is coming, tell your lender immediately rather than hoping it goes unnoticed. It will be noticed. Sometimes it is manageable with the right documentation, and only if they know in advance.

**Do not move large sums around.** Transfers between your own accounts create sourcing work for no benefit. New deposits have to be explained. See [Large Deposits and NSF Items](/blog/large-deposits-nsf-bank-statements/).

**Do not spend your reserves.** Furniture, a down payment increase to win a bidding war, covering an appraisal gap. Any of these can clear one requirement and fail another.

**Do not pay off a collection without asking.** It can affect how recent the item looks and occasionally produces a worse score. Ask before you write the check.

**Do not let anything sit.** Respond to document requests within a day. Underwriting queues are first in, first out, and a document on your desk for three days costs you far more than three days.

## How to prevent this before you write an offer

**Get fully underwritten, not pre-qualified.** The single highest-value thing on this page. Submit the complete package, have a real underwriter review it, and get a condition list. Then the only things left are the property and the final verifications, which is a dramatically smaller surface area for surprises.

**Ask what was actually reviewed.** *"Has an underwriter looked at this, or just you? What income figure are you using, and how did you calculate it?"* If they cannot show you the derivation, it has not been done.

**Volunteer your complications.** The support obligation, the old bankruptcy, the business you started last year, the rental you own with a relative. An underwriter finds everything. A problem raised on day one has solutions; the same problem found in week three has fewer.

**Ask about the overlay.** *"Is your minimum the guideline, or yours?"* If your file is near any edge, this determines whether you are one lender's decline or genuinely unqualified.

**Negotiate a closing date that reflects your file.** A self-employed jumbo on a property with a well and two associations is not a thirty day close. See [A Realistic Escrow Timeline](/blog/escrow-timeline-arizona-30-days/).

## If it has already happened

**Get the adverse action notice in writing.** You generally have a right to the specific reasons rather than a category. That notice is the difference between guessing and knowing. See [What to Do After a Mortgage Denial](/blog/after-a-mortgage-denial/).

**Find out whether the reason was you or the property.** If the appraisal, the condo project or the title killed it, nothing about you needs to change and a different property may close next week.

**Find out whether the reason was the guideline or the overlay.** If it was the overlay, the file may be approvable elsewhere today with no change at all. See [Declined by One Lender, Approved by Another](/blog/declined-by-one-lender-approved-by-another/).

**Talk to your agent about the contract immediately.** Deadlines, contingencies and what happens to earnest money are matters for your real estate broker, and the clock is running. That is their lane, not your lender's, and it is urgent.

**Do not apply in a panic at four more lenders the same week.** Shop deliberately with one person who can place the file, rather than scattering applications and compounding the problem.
