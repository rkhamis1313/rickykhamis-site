The reason most listing agents compete on price against builders who compete on payment is not that they do not understand the math. It is that advertising a rate feels legally risky, so they do not, and the builder down the street with a compliance department does.

The rules are real. They are also manageable, and working inside them is a competitive advantage precisely because so few resale listings bother.

Nothing here is legal advice. Your broker and your lender's compliance function make these calls. What follows is the practical shape of it so you know what to ask for.

## The core principle

Advertising a rate or a payment triggers disclosure obligations. The governing idea is that a consumer who sees an attractive number must also see the terms that produce it, in the same piece of advertising, clearly enough to actually read.

The failure mode regulators care about is a big number and a small asterisk. Not the existence of an asterisk.

## What generally has to accompany a payment figure

Expect your lender's compliance review to require most of the following. Get the exact language from them rather than drafting your own:

**That the figure is an example, not an offer.** Language establishing that it is illustrative and not a commitment to lend or an offer to extend credit.

**The assumptions behind it.** Loan amount, down payment, loan type, term, and occupancy. A payment with no stated loan amount is not interpretable and that is the problem with it.

**The full payment schedule, not just the first year.** On a temporary buydown, every step. First year, second year, and the payment from the step up forward. Showing only the attractive year is the single most common and most serious mistake in this kind of advertising.

**That the buyer must qualify.** Credit approval, underwriting and property eligibility apply.

**Whether the payment includes taxes, insurance and association dues.** A principal and interest figure presented as a total payment is misleading in Arizona, where property taxes and association dues are a meaningful part of the real number. Say which it is.

**The licensed entity and NMLS identifiers.** The lender's company name and NMLS ID, and the loan originator's. Your brokerage will have its own requirements on top.

## Why a locked rate makes this materially easier

There is a difference between advertising a payment derived from an assumed rate and advertising one derived from a rate that is locked and exists.

The first is a projection. If pricing moves before a buyer calls, the number in your flyer is no longer available and you have advertised something you cannot deliver. That is the exposure agents correctly worry about.

The second is a fact about a specific property. It is still accompanied by disclosures, the buyer still has to qualify, but the rate itself is not a guess.

That ordering is the design of [List & Lock](https://www.epiqlending.com/mysite/Ricky-Khamis/listnlock): the rate is locked to the address before the listing goes live, so the figure in your marketing is defensible from day one.

## Practical construction of a flyer

**Top third: the payment ladder.** Three lines, year by year, each with a dollar figure. The attractive number is first because it is the headline, and the step up is right underneath it because hiding it is both a compliance problem and a sales problem.

**Middle: the house.** Photos, beds, baths, square footage, the usual.

**One line in the body:** that the seller is funding a rate buydown and the buyer should ask for details.

**Bottom: the disclosure block.** Assumptions, illustrative language, qualifying language, NMLS identifiers, Equal Housing Opportunity. Readable type. Not four point grey on white, which defeats the purpose and is exactly what gets flagged.

**A direct phone number** for the lender. A buyer standing in the kitchen with a question is the warmest lead the listing will generate and it goes cold by Monday.

## Three rules that will keep you out of trouble

**Never publish a rate or payment your lender has not given you in writing for that property.** Not one you calculated. Not one from last month. Not a round number that seems about right.

**Never show the first year payment alone.** Always the full ladder.

**Run every piece past your broker and the lender's compliance process before it goes out.** Flyers, portal remarks, sign riders, social posts, email blasts. All of it. The turnaround is usually fast and it is the cheapest insurance in the transaction.

Compliance is why resale listings are silent on payments while builders shout about them. Once you have a locked rate and an approved disclosure block, you can run the builder's play on a finished home in an established neighborhood, and the builder's main advantage disappears.
