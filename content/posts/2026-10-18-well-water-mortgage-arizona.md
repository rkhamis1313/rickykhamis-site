North of Scottsdale, through Cave Creek, Carefree, Rio Verde and the unincorporated county land in between, a large number of parcels get their water from a well rather than a municipal connection. Buyers from out of state frequently treat this as a quirk. Lenders treat it as collateral eligibility, which is a different thing entirely.

Here is what has to be documented and where these files actually stall.

## The underlying requirement

A lender needs the property to have a water supply that is **sufficient, potable, and legally available to the property.** That is the whole standard. Every specific requirement below exists to prove one of those three things.

A parcel whose water supply cannot be documented to the investor's satisfaction is a parcel some investors will decline, no matter how strong the borrower is. This is a property issue, not a borrower issue, and a 790 credit score does not solve it.

## Private well: what gets requested

**Evidence the well exists and is registered.** Arizona wells are registered with the Arizona Department of Water Resources, and a registration record tied to the parcel is the starting point.

**Potability testing.** A water quality test from an accepted laboratory, with results inside the parameters the program requires. Which contaminants get tested and at what thresholds varies by program, and local requirements can add to the list.

**Flow or yield testing, where required.** Some programs want evidence the well produces enough water to serve the house. A low-producing well is a real finding and occasionally an expensive one.

**Location and separation.** Distance between the well and the septic system matters, and there are minimum separation standards. A well too close to a leach field is a finding.

**Confirmation the well serves this parcel legally.** Which sounds obvious until you meet a parcel where the well is physically on a neighbor's land.

Timing note that matters: **test results have a shelf life.** Tests completed too early in a long escrow can go stale and need redoing. Coordinate the timing with your lender rather than knocking it out on day two and assuming it is handled.

## Shared wells: the part that stalls files

A shared well serves multiple parcels, and this is where most well-related delays happen.

Investors generally require a **recorded shared well agreement** that addresses the substantive questions: who owns the well, how maintenance and repair costs are allocated, how water is allocated among the parcels, what access rights each parcel has to the equipment, and what happens when a party refuses to pay or the well fails.

Two things go wrong:

**There is no recorded agreement.** Neighbors have operated on a handshake for twenty years. That works socially and fails underwriting. Creating and recording an agreement mid-escrow requires every affected owner to sign, which is a negotiation you do not control on a timeline you do not control.

**There is an agreement and it does not satisfy the investor.** It exists, it is recorded, and it is silent on something the investor requires. Different investors have different views on what is adequate, which means a file declined by one may be approved by another.

**The instruction:** get a copy of the shared well agreement before you make an offer. Not during escrow. Before. Send it to your lender and let them tell you whether it works.

## Hauled water

Some parcels, notably in the Rio Verde Foothills area, have historically depended on water delivered by truck to a storage tank rather than a well or a service connection. That arrangement became a well-publicized problem when the supply route changed, and while arrangements have evolved since, the financing reality is stable: **a hauled water parcel has substantially fewer financing options and some investors will not lend at all.**

If a property you are considering relies on hauled water, raise it on your first lender call, before you tour it twice. Sometimes cash is the only path, and that should be a knowing decision rather than a week-three discovery. See [Buying a Second Home in Rio Verde](/blog/second-home-rio-verde-az/).

## The appraisal interaction

An appraiser notes the water source and comments on it. Two consequences:

The appraiser may condition the appraisal on documentation, which means the loan cannot clear until the testing is complete and acceptable.

Water source affects value and the comparable selection. A well-served parcel and a city-served parcel are not identical collateral, and an appraiser should be adjusting for it.

## Where this fits with everything else out there

A well rarely travels alone. Parcels on wells are usually also on septic, usually on larger acreage, and frequently reached by a private road. Each of those is its own item:

- **Septic** means the Arizona transfer of ownership inspection. See [The Arizona Septic Transfer Inspection](/blog/arizona-septic-transfer-inspection-loan/).
- **Acreage** stretches the comparable sales set and can trigger land-to-value limits.
- **Private road access** requires legal recorded access, and sometimes a maintenance agreement.

And if your income file is also non-standard, understand the compounding: **not every non-QM investor lends on well-served acreage.** An unusual borrower plus an unusual property narrows the field fast. See [Self-Employed in Cave Creek](/blog/self-employed-cave-creek/).

## The checklist, in order

1. **Ask the listing agent the water source** before you tour a second time.
2. **If shared, get the recorded agreement** and send it to your lender immediately.
3. **If hauled, call your lender before you write.**
4. **Put testing on the escrow calendar at contract**, with timing coordinated so results do not go stale.
5. **Ask about well and septic separation.**
6. **Confirm the well is legally on and serving your parcel.**
7. **Confirm legal recorded access** to the property while you are at it.
8. **Expect this to add days**, and build them into the closing date rather than discovering them.
