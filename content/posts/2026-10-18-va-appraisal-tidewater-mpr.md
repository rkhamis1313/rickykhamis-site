Two things make a VA appraisal different from a conventional one. One of them is a reputation problem and the other is an advantage nobody uses.

## Minimum property requirements

[[RULE:va-minimum-property-requirements]]

Like FHA, VA assesses whether the property is safe, structurally sound and sanitary, not only what it is worth. The appraiser is looking at condition as well as value, and items that fail can require correction before the loan funds.

On Arizona housing the recurring ones look much like the FHA list: roof condition and remaining life, peeling paint on older homes, exposed wiring, non-functioning heating or cooling, plumbing leaks, missing handrails, broken windows, pool condition and barriers, evidence of termite damage, and water intrusion or grading that directs water at the foundation.

What is **not** required, despite the folklore: cosmetic condition, dated finishes, or bringing an older home up to current code. The standard is safety, soundness and sanitation, not taste. A tired but sound house generally passes.

See [What an FHA Appraiser Will Make the Seller Fix](/blog/fha-appraisal-repairs-sellers/), because the practical overlap is large and the preparation is identical.

## Tidewater, which is the part worth knowing

[[RULE:va-appraisal-tidewater]]

Here is what VA does that nobody else does.

When a VA appraiser is forming an opinion of value that will come in **below the contract price**, there is a process by which notice is given before the value is finalised, creating an opportunity to supply additional comparable sales and relevant market data for consideration.

Think about what that means. On a conventional or FHA appraisal, a low value arrives as a finished document and your only recourse is a reconsideration after the fact, which rarely changes anything. On a VA file there is a window **before** the number is issued.

Three consequences:

**Your agent needs to be ready.** The window is short and it is for data, not arguments. Sales that are genuinely more comparable, with a reason each is better. Pending sales and relevant market evidence. Documented improvements with dates and costs.

**Your lender has to know to act.** The notice comes through the lender and the appraisal management chain. An originator who has not closed VA loans may not recognize what they are looking at or may not get the information to the right place in time.

**Most listing agents have never heard of it.** Which means on a VA offer, having somebody in the transaction who has is worth real money. It is one of the few places in lending where preparation directly changes an outcome.

Ask your lender directly: *"If Tidewater comes back on this file, what is your process and how fast do you need comps from my agent?"* The answer tells you a great deal about who you are working with.

## If the value still comes in low

A Notice of Value below the contract price is not the end. The options look like the ones on any file, with a VA-specific wrinkle:

- The **seller reduces** to the appraised value.
- The **buyer covers the gap in cash**, keeping in mind that VA financing is sized on the lower of price or value.
- The parties **split it**.
- A **reconsideration of value** is pursued with genuinely better comparable sales.

See [When the Appraisal Comes In Low](/blog/appraisal-gap-arizona-contract/) for how gaps get closed generally, and talk to your broker about contract deadlines, which are their lane and not your lender's.

## For listing agents taking a VA offer

Most of the risk is removable before you ever receive one.

**Walk the property against the condition list.** Paint on older homes, handrails, cover plates, the pool, a visibly failing roof. Cheap now, expensive mid-escrow.

**Have your comparable sales ready** in case Tidewater notice comes back. If you priced the home properly you already did this work; you just need it in a form you can send in a day.

**Do not reject VA offers on reputation.** A veteran with entitlement, no down payment requirement and no mortgage insurance is frequently a stronger closing risk than a stretched conventional buyer, and the appraisal items are usually a few hundred to a few thousand dollars.

**Remember the loan is assumable**, which is a selling point for the buyer's eventual exit and a reason the property may trade well later. See [Your VA Loan Is Assumable](/blog/va-assumable-loan-advantage/).
