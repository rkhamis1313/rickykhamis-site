Buyers see the closing cost figure for the first time on a Loan Estimate and react to the total. The total is less useful than the breakdown, because the lines behave very differently. Some are set by the lender and negotiable. Some are fixed by a government body. Some are customarily allocated by local practice and get negotiated in the contract.

Here is how to read it.

## The categories that matter

Loan Estimates and Closing Disclosures group charges into sections, and the grouping tells you something important: **which charges you can shop for.**

**Origination charges.** The lender's own fees: origination, underwriting, processing, and any discount points. These are the lender's to set, and they are the primary thing you are comparing between lenders. A lower rate with higher origination charges may or may not be a better deal than the reverse, which is exactly why comparing total cost beats comparing rate.

**Services you cannot shop for.** Items the lender selects, commonly the appraisal, credit reports, flood certification and tax service. You are stuck with the lender's vendor, which means these are part of the lender comparison too.

**Services you can shop for.** Typically title insurance and settlement services. Your lender must give you a list of providers, and you may use someone else. In practice most Arizona buyers use the escrow and title company named in the contract, which is fine, and worth knowing that you could price it.

**Government charges.** Recording fees and any applicable transfer charges. Fixed. Nobody is negotiating these.

**Prepaids.** Interest from closing to the end of the month, the initial homeowner's insurance premium, and in some cases prepaid property taxes. These are not fees, they are costs of ownership paid in advance, and they vary with your closing date. Closing late in the month reduces prepaid interest, which is why the figure moves when the date moves.

**Escrow reserves.** The initial deposit into your impound account for taxes and insurance, where you have one. Also not a fee. It is your money, held to pay your bills.

## Arizona specifics worth knowing

**Property taxes are billed in two installments** and the proration at closing follows the county's cycle. Arizona's tax calendar does not align neatly with a calendar year, and the proration arithmetic surprises people routinely. Your escrow officer handles it; what matters is understanding that an unexpected number here is usually correct and not a mistake.

**Arizona does not impose a real estate transfer tax**, which buyers coming from states that do find to be a pleasant discovery. There are still recording fees and an affidavit of value requirement.

**Escrow and title practice is well established here**, and most transactions run through a title agency handling both. The allocation of who pays the owner's title policy versus the lender's policy is a matter of local custom and contract negotiation rather than law.

**Homeowner association transfer and disclosure fees** are a real line item and they vary by association. On a property with multiple associations, which plenty of Scottsdale addresses have, there may be several. See [When HOA Documents Delay a Closing](/blog/hoa-document-delays-closing/).

**Septic transfer inspection** is a cost, and a scheduling obligation, on any property with an on-site system. See [The Arizona Septic Transfer Inspection](/blog/arizona-septic-transfer-inspection-loan/).

**Well testing** where applicable. See [Well Water and Mortgage Financing in Arizona](/blog/well-water-mortgage-arizona/).

Who customarily pays what is partly local practice and substantially a matter of contract negotiation. That is your real estate broker's expertise, and the contract governs, not custom.

## Where the real money is

Three places, in order of size:

**1. Discount points and the rate.** This is the biggest number on the page and the one buyers pay the least attention to, because it is expressed as a rate rather than a dollar figure. The decision of how much to pay upfront versus accept in the rate is worth more than every other line combined. Ask your lender to show you the same loan at two or three pricing points so you can see the trade.

**2. Lender fees.** Compare them between lenders. They are not standardized and the spread is real.

**3. Seller contributions.** The most underused lever. A seller paying your closing costs frees your cash for down payment or reserves, and the same dollars could instead fund a rate buydown that reduces your payment far more than a closing cost credit does. That comparison is worth running explicitly. See [Seller Concession vs Price Reduction](/blog/seller-concession-vs-price-reduction-math/) and note the [contribution caps](/blog/seller-concession-limits-by-loan-type/) that limit how much help you may accept.

## How to compare two lenders properly

**Get Loan Estimates, not worksheets.** The Loan Estimate is standardized so that offers can be compared. A lender who will give you a rate but not a Loan Estimate is a lender giving you a number you cannot check.

**Compare on identical terms.** Same loan amount, same down payment, same program, same lock period. A thirty day quote against a sixty day quote is not a comparison.

**Compare total cost, not rate.** Add origination charges, points and lender fees, then look at the rate. A quarter point lower rate bought with substantially higher fees may lose over your actual holding period.

**Get them the same day.** Pricing moves daily. Estimates from different weeks tell you nothing.

**Watch the lock period and the prepaids.** A lender can make a total look smaller by assuming a closing date that minimizes prepaid interest, which is not a real saving.

## Reducing what you actually pay

- **Negotiate a seller contribution**, and decide deliberately whether it goes to closing costs or a buydown.
- **Shop the lender**, properly, with same-day Loan Estimates on identical terms.
- **Shop title and settlement** if you want to, using the provider list.
- **Consider the rate and points trade-off** against how long you will realistically hold the loan.
- **Ask about lender credits**, which raise your rate in exchange for covering costs. Sometimes the right trade, frequently not, always worth seeing.
- **Mind your closing date** if prepaid interest matters to your cash at closing.

## What not to do

Do not allocate your last liquid dollars to closing costs. **Reserves are tested after closing**, and on jumbo and non-QM files they are a hard requirement rather than a preference. A buyer who clears the closing table with nothing left occasionally fails underwriting for exactly that reason.
