FHA or Conventional: How to Actually Decide, Not Which One Is Better

By Ricky Khamis · October 18, 2026 · 5 min read

Verified and written by Ricky Khamis, President of EPiQ Lending, lending in Arizona since 1999. NMLS #173141 · EPiQ Lending NMLS #1936984 · Scottsdale, AZ. Every guideline on this page carries its source and the date it was verified.(480) 999-9842

Part of the FHA loans in Arizona guide, where every rule on this page is cited and dated.

The question gets asked as though one program wins. It does not. These are two different structures and the right one depends on your credit, your down payment, the property, and how long you intend to keep the loan.

Here is the actual decision framework, and the factor that most often decides it.

The structural difference

FHA is a government insurance program. HUD insures a loan an approved lender makes. Because the loan is insured, lenders can accept a weaker credit profile and a smaller down payment than they otherwise would. You pay for that insurance, both upfront and annually.

Conventional is not government insured. The loan conforms to the standards of Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac so it can be sold to them. With less than twenty percent down you generally carry private mortgage insurance, which is priced on your credit and your loan-to-value.

Both are real mortgages from real lenders. The difference is who absorbs the risk and how you pay for that.

The factor that usually decides it

Mortgage insurance duration, and it is the thing borrowers weigh least and should weigh most.

Conventional private mortgage insurance is generally cancellable. As you build equity, it can come off, and the loan continues without it. There are rules about when and how, and they are worth knowing, but the premise is that it ends.

FHA mortgage insurance may not come off. Depending on the terms of your specific loan, the annual premium can run for a very long period or for the life of the loan, and the only reliable way out is to refinance into a different program entirely.

How long do I pay FHA mortgage insurance? The upfront premium is 175 basis points, 1.75%, of the base loan amount, financed or paid in cash. The annual premium runs for a period set by your original loan-to-value: at 90% or below it is assessed for the first 11 years of the term or until the end of the term, whichever comes first; above 90%, which includes every minimum-down-payment file, it is assessed for 30 years or the end of the term. This applies to case numbers assigned on or after June 3, 2013. Above 90% the only reliable exit is refinancing out of FHA.

That distinction compounds. Over a long hold, insurance that ends and insurance that does not are a materially different total cost, even when the monthly figures start out similar. See How Long You Pay FHA Mortgage Insurance.

So the honest framing is: FHA buys you access now, and you may pay for that access for a long time unless you refinance out. For a lot of borrowers that is a completely reasonable trade. For a borrower who could have gone conventional, it is frequently a bad one.

Where FHA is clearly the better answer

Your credit profile is thinner than conventional wants. This is the program's purpose and it does the job well. Conventional pricing and mortgage insurance both deteriorate sharply as credit weakens, to the point where FHA becomes cheaper in total even with the insurance disadvantage.

What credit score does FHA actually require? A minimum decision credit score of 580 reaches FHA's maximum financing, a 96.50% loan-to-value on a purchase. Scores from 500 to 579 are eligible at a reduced loan-to-value, which means a larger down payment. The rate-and-term and simple refinance maximum is 97.75% loan-to-value, and cash-out is capped at 80%.

What lenders actually do. The lender matrix I verified this against sets its own floor at 580 for every transaction type and offers no 500 to 579 tier at all, and it also requires an automated underwriting approval. So FHA's published floor of 500 is not available there at any down payment. That is the overlay, and it is why the number you read online is not your real minimum. Where conventional is clearly the better answer

Your credit is strong. At good scores, conventional pricing and cancellable mortgage insurance beat FHA comfortably over any meaningful hold.

You are putting down enough to avoid mortgage insurance entirely. No contest.

You are buying a condo that is not FHA approved. Conventional project review is a different and frequently easier path. See Does the Condo Have to Be FHA Approved?.

You are buying a second home or an investment property. FHA is an owner-occupant program. This is not a preference, it is the premise.

You expect to hold the loan a long time and would rather not depend on a future refinance to shed the insurance.

You are competing against other offers. Some listing agents treat an FHA offer as carrying more appraisal risk because of minimum property requirements, fairly or not. On a contested property that perception has a cost. See What an FHA Appraiser Will Make the Seller Fix.

How to decide, concretely

Do not decide from an article, including this one. Ask your lender for this, in writing, on your actual numbers:

  1. The total monthly payment on each, principal, interest, taxes, insurance, association dues and mortgage insurance, stated separately so you can see the insurance line.
  2. Total cash to close on each, including the FHA upfront premium.
  3. How long the mortgage insurance lasts on each, specifically. On conventional, when it can be cancelled. On FHA, whether it ever comes off under your loan's terms.
  4. Total cost over the period you realistically expect to keep the loan, not over thirty years. If you move or refinance in five years, the thirty year comparison is irrelevant.
  5. Whether you qualify for both. Sometimes this is academic because only one is available, and knowing that saves the analysis.

Then look at line 3 and line 4 together. That is usually where the answer is.

Two things people get wrong

"FHA is for first-time buyers." It is not restricted to first-time buyers. It is an owner-occupant program, and repeat buyers use it.

"I will just refinance out of the FHA insurance later." Maybe. That depends on rates and on your equity at the time, neither of which you control. Treat it as an option, never as the plan. A structure that only works if you refinance is a structure that has not accounted for rates staying where they are.

If you are genuinely close

Then the deciding factor is usually credit, and credit is the one variable you can still move. Sixty to ninety days of focused work can shift you from the FHA case into the conventional case, which over a long hold is worth more than almost anything else available to you.

That is worth knowing before you shop. See Fix the Credit First and Pre-Qualified, Pre-Approved, Underwritten.
