Builder's Lender or Your Own: How to Compare Without Losing the Incentive

By Ricky Khamis · October 23, 2026 · 4 min read

You are standing in a sales office in the north valley. The incentive on the table is substantial and it is conditioned on using the builder's mortgage company. The sales agent is pleasant and the number on the wall is attractive.

Here is how to evaluate that offer properly, because it is frequently a good deal and it is frequently not, and the only way to know is to do a comparison most buyers never do.

Why the incentive is tied to their lender

Not a conspiracy, just economics. The builder has an affiliated mortgage company, and the incentive budget they spend on your financing largely stays inside the organization. Tying the incentive to their lender means the money buys a sale and captures the loan revenue at the same time.

It also eliminates your leverage. A buyer who cannot take the incentive elsewhere cannot make two lenders compete, and the price of a loan with no competition is, on average, worse. That is not a claim about anyone's integrity. It is how pricing works everywhere.

So the question is not whether the builder's lender is good. The question is whether the incentive exceeds the cost of not shopping.

You have the right to use your own lender

Worth stating plainly because the sales office presentation can leave a different impression. Federal law prohibits a seller of a property financed by a federally related mortgage loan from requiring the buyer to use a particular title company, and the broader framework around affiliated business arrangements requires disclosure and prohibits requiring use of an affiliate as a condition of the sale.

What a builder can do is condition an incentive on using their lender. That is generally permitted. So the practical situation is: you may use your own lender, and you may lose some or all of the incentive if you do.

Which makes it a math problem.

The comparison to actually run

Get both offers in writing and compare the total cost, not the rate.

1. Get the builder lender's Loan Estimate. Not a worksheet, not a flyer, the Loan Estimate. It is a standardized form and it exists so that offers can be compared.

2. Get an outside lender's Loan Estimate on the same loan amount, same down payment, same program, same lock period. The same lock period matters enormously on new construction and it is where comparisons get distorted.

3. Put these side by side:

  • The note rate, and whether it is a permanent rate or a temporary buydown
  • Total points and origination charges
  • Total lender fees in section A
  • Third party costs, noting which the builder is covering
  • The incentive, stated as a dollar amount
  • The lock period, and the cost of extending it
  • Mortgage insurance, if applicable

4. Do the arithmetic. Outside lender's total cost, minus builder lender's total cost, compared against the incentive you give up. If the incentive is larger than the cost difference, take the builder's lender. If it is smaller, use your own and keep the better loan.

That is the whole analysis and almost nobody does it, which is why the incentive works as well as it does.

The three distortions to watch for

The rate on the wall is a temporary buydown, not a permanent rate. A first year rate in the fives, stepping up afterward to a note rate near market, is a different product from a permanent rate in the fives. Both are legitimate. They are not comparable. Ask: is this the note rate, or a subsidized rate for a period? See How a 2-1 Buydown Actually Works.

The lock period is not the same. A rate quoted on a thirty day lock, for a house delivering in seven months, is a quote for something you cannot use. The comparison has to be on a lock that survives the delivery timeline, and extended locks cost money. See Extended Rate Locks on New Construction.

The incentive is bundled. Frequently a single number covers financing help, design center credit and closing costs. Ask for it broken out. Design center credit is not loan pricing and should not be compared against it.

Where an outside lender frequently wins

Your file is not standard. Self-employed income, a bank statement program, asset depletion, substantial K-1 income, foreign income, recent credit events. Builder-affiliated lenders are generally efficient at conventional and agency files and have a narrower shelf for everything else. A broker with many investors has a wider one. If your income is complicated, the incentive may not be worth being inside a narrow box.

You need a long lock with a float-down. Terms on extended locks vary meaningfully between lenders.

You are in jumbo territory. Jumbo pricing varies more between investors than conforming pricing does, so shopping is worth more.

The incentive is modest. Some are large. Some are small enough that the pricing difference exceeds them.

The move that gets you both

Bring an outside lender in before you sign the purchase agreement, not after.

The builder's incentive is a negotiating position, and the sales office has heard "I have a better offer" before. Occasionally they will match, extend, or restructure. Occasionally they will not and you decide with full information. Either way you are deciding rather than accepting.

What you lose by waiting: once the agreement is signed with the incentive conditioned as written, your leverage is gone.

Before you sign in a sales office

  • Ask for the Loan Estimate, not a worksheet.
  • Ask whether the advertised rate is permanent or temporary, and get the full payment ladder.
  • Ask what lock period the quote assumes and what an extension costs.
  • Ask for the incentive broken out between financing, design center and closing costs.
  • Get a competing Loan Estimate on identical terms including the same lock period.
  • Do the subtraction. Cost difference versus incentive forgone.
  • If your income file is non-standard, shop before you sign. That is where the real money is.
