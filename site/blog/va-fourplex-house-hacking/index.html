The Fourplex Play: How a Veteran Can Own 16 Doors in 10 Years With No Money Down

By Ricky Khamis · October 18, 2026 · 6 min read

Verified and written by Ricky Khamis, President of EPiQ Lending, lending in Arizona since 1999. NMLS #173141 · EPiQ Lending NMLS #1936984 · Scottsdale, AZ. Every guideline on this page carries its source and the date it was verified.(480) 999-9842

Part of the VA loans in Arizona guide, where every rule on this page is cited and dated.

Most veterans use their VA benefit once, on a single-family house, and never think about it again. That is a reasonable life decision and it leaves an enormous amount on the table.

The VA loan will finance up to four units with no down payment, provided you live in one of them. That one sentence is the foundation of the most powerful wealth-building tool available to anyone who has served, and almost nobody explains it.

Here is the whole play, the arithmetic, and an honest account of what breaks it.

The structure

A fourplex has four units. You occupy one. You rent the other three.

The tenants in those three units pay most of your mortgage. You live in a property worth four times what you could otherwise afford, with nothing down, no monthly mortgage insurance, and a note that somebody can assume from you later.

Then after a couple of years you refinance out of the VA loan into conventional financing, which restores your entitlement, and you do it again on a second fourplex.

Read the last line again. You are living in a $900,000 asset for less than a decent two-bedroom rental costs in this market, and the tenants are paying down your principal every month.

That is the entire idea. Everything after this is repetition.

The sequence over ten years

Roughly a two-year cycle. Buy, occupy, let the rents season, refinance into conventional financing so the VA loan is paid off and the entitlement comes back, move into the next one, repeat.

Four properties, sixteen doors, inside a decade. Then you stop, keep fifteen rented units throwing off income, and go buy the house you actually want to live in.

Why conventional on the refinance? Because the VA loan requires you to occupy. Once you move out, you are no longer eligible to keep that property on VA financing indefinitely, and conventional investment financing is the correct home for an asset you no longer live in. The refinance also pays off the VA loan, which is what frees the entitlement.

The four things that break this

I am not going to sell you the version where this always works. Here is what actually goes wrong.

1. You have to move. Four times. The occupancy requirement is not a formality, and it is the real cost of this strategy. Every purchase means relocating your household into a unit of a building you just bought. If you have a family settled in a school, that is a serious conversation and sometimes the answer is no. This plan is far easier at 26 than at 46.

2. The rents might not cover it. My table assumed three units at $1,600 and full occupancy. Reality brings vacancy between tenants, a tenant who stops paying, turnover costs, and the capital expenses nobody budgets for. A roof, two air conditioners and a sewer line on a sixteen-unit portfolio are not hypothetical, they are a Tuesday. Underwrite every one of these assuming a vacant unit and a repair, not a full house and a quiet year.

3. The refinance has to be available. Refinancing a four-unit investment property is a different proposition from refinancing a house. The loan-to-value limits are lower, the pricing is higher, and the property's income has to support it. If values have not moved and rates are higher than when you bought, the refinance that frees your entitlement may not be there when you want it. A strategy that only works if you can refinance is a strategy that has not planned for rates staying put.

4. Sixteen doors is a business. Not passive income. It is leases, maintenance calls, turnovers, bookkeeping, fair housing compliance and the occasional eviction. You either do that work or you pay a manager eight to ten percent of gross to do it, which has to be in your numbers from the first property.

Buy the first one and live in it for the required period before you plan the second. The single most common way this fails is a veteran planning four purchases before completing one.

The honest summary

The mechanism is real, it is written into the program, and it is available to anyone with entitlement and the willingness to move a few times. Veterans do build substantial portfolios this way.

It is not passive, it is not guaranteed, and it depends on rents, values and refinance availability that nobody controls. Anyone presenting this as certain wealth is selling something.

What I will do is run your actual numbers on an actual building and tell you whether the first one works. That is the only part that matters right now.
