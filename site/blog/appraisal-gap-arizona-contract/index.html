When the Appraisal Comes In Low: Your Options on an Arizona Contract

By Ricky Khamis · October 24, 2026 · 5 min read

The appraisal came back under the contract price. Nobody is happy, and the transaction is not dead. What has happened is narrower than it feels: the lender will lend against the appraised value rather than the price, which means somebody has to cover the difference in cash or the price has to move.

Here is the full set of options and how to think about them.

Why the lender cares about value, not price

A mortgage is secured by the property. The lender sizes the loan as a percentage of value, and when price and appraised value differ, the lower of the two is used. That is not a negotiating position, it is how the loan is sized.

So a gap between price and value is always covered by cash, from someone. The question is whose.

The five ways a gap gets closed

1. The buyer brings the difference in cash.

The loan stays at the same percentage of the lower value, and the buyer increases their down payment by the gap. This is the cleanest path when the buyer has the money and genuinely wants the house.

The thing to verify before agreeing to it: does the buyer still satisfy the reserve requirement afterward? Reserves are tested after closing. A buyer who covers a gap with their last liquid dollars can clear the down payment and fail the reserve test, which is a worse outcome than the gap itself. Run that before committing.

2. The seller reduces the price to the appraised value.

Also clean, and the seller's instinct is to refuse it. Worth a calm conversation, because the appraisal is information about what the next buyer's lender will likely also conclude. A seller who refuses and goes back to market frequently meets the same value with a different buyer, having lost six weeks.

3. The parties split it.

Most common outcome in practice. Partly a math exercise, mostly a relationship exercise between the two agents.

4. The structure changes.

Sometimes the gap is solvable without anyone bringing new money, by changing the loan. A different program, a different loan-to-value tier, or a different mortgage insurance structure can occasionally absorb some of the difference. This is worth asking your lender about explicitly rather than assuming the first structure is the only structure.

5. A reconsideration of value.

Challenge the appraisal. Which is a real process with real requirements, and not a matter of complaining.

How a reconsideration of value actually works

A reconsideration is a request that the appraiser review specific, factual information they may not have had. It is not an argument that the number should be higher because the contract says so.

What has a chance of working:

  • Closed sales the appraiser did not use, that are genuinely more comparable: closer, more similar in size and age, more similar in condition, and closed recently. You need to say why each one is better, specifically.
  • Factual errors. Wrong square footage, wrong bedroom count, wrong lot size, a missed permitted addition, a missed garage conversion, wrong condition rating based on something demonstrably inaccurate.
  • Material improvements the appraiser did not know about, with documentation. A new roof, a remodeled kitchen, a replaced HVAC system, solar that is owned rather than leased.

What does not work: the contract price, the list price, how much the buyer loves it, what the seller paid, what Zillow says, or an unsupported assertion that the market is hotter than the data shows.

Practical notes: the request goes through the lender, not directly to the appraiser, because appraiser independence rules govern that communication. Your agent assembles the comparable sales, since that is their expertise. And the appraiser can decline to change anything, which is frequently what happens. Treat a reconsideration as a possibility, not a plan.

The contract side, which is your broker's call not mine

Arizona transactions commonly address appraisal contingencies in the purchase contract, and how a specific contract handles a low appraisal, what notice is required, by when, and what remedies exist, is a matter for your real estate broker and where appropriate your attorney. I am a lender; contract interpretation is not my lane and you should be suspicious of any lender who tells you otherwise.

What I will say is that appraisal gap language has become common in competitive situations, where a buyer commits in advance to cover a gap up to a stated amount. If you are considering agreeing to that, the lender-side question is simple and important: can you actually produce that cash and still satisfy your reserve requirement? Get that answered before you sign, not after.

How to reduce the odds of a gap

Order the appraisal early. On a resale this is a matter of days. It also leaves room to solve a problem rather than discovering it at the deadline.

Make sure the appraiser knows the market. This matters enormously in low-volume, high-variability areas. An appraiser who works mostly in production subdivisions, handed a custom home on acreage in Carefree or a view lot in Fountain Hills, has a harder job and a wider margin of error. A broker can raise this; a retail lender with one appraisal panel often cannot. See Self-Employed in Carefree.

Give the appraiser information. A list of improvements with dates and costs, and the comparable sales your agent relied on in pricing. This is permitted and it is useful. It is not pressure, it is data.

Expect more risk in specific situations: custom homes, large acreage, unusual view premiums, significant equestrian improvements, condominiums in buildings with few recent sales, new construction where the appraisal comes late in the build, and any property where the contract price was set in a bidding situation.

If you are buying new construction

The appraisal is frequently ordered close to delivery, months after the price was agreed. The market may have moved. Ask the builder what happens if the appraisal comes in low, and get the answer before you sign, because the purchase agreement may address it in a way that is not in your favor. See Extended Rate Locks on New Construction.

Before you need this post

  • Know your cash position beyond the down payment, including what remains for reserves.
  • Decide your gap tolerance before you write, in a dollar figure.
  • Ask your lender whether a structure change could absorb a gap on your file.
  • Order the appraisal early.
  • Ask who the appraiser is and whether they work your submarket, particularly on unusual property.
  • Have your agent ready to assemble comparables if a reconsideration is worth attempting.
