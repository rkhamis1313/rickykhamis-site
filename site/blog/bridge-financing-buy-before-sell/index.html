Buying Before You Sell: Bridge Loans and the Three Alternatives

By Ricky Khamis · October 28, 2026 · 5 min read

The problem is almost universal above a certain price point. You own a house with substantial equity, you want a different house, and the equity you need for the new purchase is locked in the one you are leaving.

Selling first is clean financially and disruptive practically. Buying first is comfortable practically and complicated financially. Here are the four structures that solve it.

First, the question that determines everything

Can you qualify carrying both payments?

An underwriter generally has to count the payment on your departing residence unless it is sold and closed, or unless specific conditions are met allowing it to be excluded. If your income supports both payments, your options are wide open and most of this post is unnecessary. If it does not, your options narrow to structures that address exactly that problem.

Get this answered first. It takes one conversation and it determines which of the four paths below is even available to you.

A related point worth asking about: program treatment of a departing residence varies. Some programs will exclude the departing payment with an executed lease and documented receipt of a security deposit. Some require the sale to have closed. Some allow rental income from the departing residence to offset it, at a haircut. These differences are large and they are investor-specific, which is a genuine argument for a broker over a single lender. See Rental Income From a Departing Residence.

Option one: a bridge loan

Short term financing secured by your current home, your new home, or both, giving you the cash for a down payment now and repaid when the old house sells.

What it does well. You buy without a sale contingency, which in a competitive situation is worth real money. You move once. You can prepare and show the old house empty, which frequently sells it faster and for more.

What it costs. Short term money is expensive relative to a thirty year mortgage: higher rate, origination costs, and in some structures interest-only payments for the term. You are also carrying two properties, so taxes, insurance, utilities and maintenance on both.

What to confirm before committing: the term and what happens if the old house has not sold when it expires, the prepayment terms, whether payments are required during the term or accrue, and how the lender treats the combined exposure for qualifying.

Who it fits. Someone with strong equity, a house that will sell, and a specific purchase they do not want to lose. The risk is a slower sale than expected, so build a plan for that rather than assuming.

Option two: a HELOC or second on your current home, opened in advance

Draw on your existing equity for the down payment, then pay it off from the sale.

What it does well. Frequently cheaper than a bridge loan. Flexible, since you draw only what you need.

The catch that ruins this one: you have to open it before you list. Most lenders will not originate a HELOC on a property that is listed for sale, and some will freeze or reduce a line if the property goes on the market. A homeowner who decides to move and then applies for a HELOC has frequently already missed the window.

If you are eighteen months from a move, this is the single most useful preparatory step available to you. Open the line while the house is not for sale and you have the option in your pocket.

Qualifying note. The HELOC payment counts in your ratio on the new purchase, so this helps with cash and not with qualifying.

Option three: buy with a sale contingency

Write the offer contingent on your house selling.

What it does well. No bridge cost, no double payments, no risk of carrying two houses.

What it costs. Competitiveness. A contingent offer is worth measurably less to a seller than a non-contingent one, and in any situation with multiple offers it generally loses. On a listing that has sat, it may be perfectly acceptable. On anything desirable, it is a disadvantage you pay for in price or in losing the house.

Strengthen it where you can: list your house first and get it under contract before you write, so the contingency is on a closing rather than on a sale that has not happened. That is a substantially better position and costs you nothing but sequence.

Option four: pay cash, then finance afterward

If you have liquid assets or borrowing capacity against a portfolio, buy with cash and put a mortgage on the new house after closing.

What it does well. The strongest possible offer. No appraisal contingency, no financing contingency, fast close. In a competitive situation this wins houses that money alone would not.

What to know. Delayed financing has specific requirements and timing rules, and they are worth understanding before you close rather than after. See Cash Offer Then Delayed Financing.

Also worth knowing: borrowing against a securities portfolio to fund a purchase has its own risks, including a demand for additional collateral if markets move against you. Your financial advisor, not your lender, should weigh in on that.

The structures that help when qualifying is the problem

If the answer to the opening question was no, you cannot carry both payments, these are the tools:

Rental income from the departing residence, where documented per program requirements, can offset the payment. An executed lease and a documented security deposit is the usual evidence.

Asset depletion, deriving qualifying income from your liquid portfolio rather than earnings. Frequently the answer for a move-up buyer who is asset-rich and income-light on paper. See Asset Depletion and Asset Qualifier Loans.

A larger down payment from a bridge or a HELOC, reducing the new payment enough to fit.

Non-QM structures where the agency ratio tests do not apply the same way, including no-ratio financing where no debt-to-income test is applied at all.

The sequence I would recommend

  1. Get underwritten before you do anything, with both payments counted, so you know your actual position. See Pre-Qualified, Pre-Approved, Underwritten.
  2. Open a HELOC on your current home now if a move is anywhere in your plans, because the window closes when you list.
  3. Price the bridge option so you know the number rather than guessing.
  4. Ask specifically how your lender treats a departing residence, and whether a lease would let you exclude the payment.
  5. Decide which structure fits before you fall in love with a house, because the structure determines how competitive your offer can be.
  6. If you are selling to buy, get the sale under contract first where you can. It is the cheapest way to strengthen a contingent offer.
