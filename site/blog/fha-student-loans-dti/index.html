How FHA Counts Your Student Loans, and Why It Changed What You Qualify For

By Ricky Khamis · October 18, 2026 · 4 min read

Verified and written by Ricky Khamis, President of EPiQ Lending, lending in Arizona since 1999. NMLS #173141 · EPiQ Lending NMLS #1936984 · Scottsdale, AZ. Every guideline on this page carries its source and the date it was verified.(480) 999-9842

Part of the FHA loans in Arizona guide, where every rule on this page is cited and dated.

If you carry student debt, this single rule probably moves your approval more than anything else in your file. It decides what monthly figure goes into your debt-to-income ratio, and your ratio is where FHA approvals are won and lost.

The rule

How is a student loan payment counted if it is deferred or on an income-driven plan? Regardless of payment status, including deferment and forbearance, the monthly obligation counted is either the payment shown on the credit report or the actual documented payment when that is above zero, or 0.5 percent of the outstanding loan balance when the reported payment is zero. The payment can be excluded entirely only with written documentation from the servicer or program that the balance has been forgiven, cancelled, discharged or paid in full. Deferment does not mean excluded.

What lenders actually do. The practical effect is the half-percent rule. A borrower deferred at zero has 0.5 percent of the balance counted against them, which on a six-figure balance is a payment far above what they pay today. That single line decides how much house a lot of people qualify for, and it is why documenting an actual payment above zero with the servicer can be worth more than anything else in the file. That is my observation from running these files, not a published requirement, and it varies by investor.Source: HUD Handbook 4000.1, verified against Part F of a wholesale FHA guideline current to 2026-10-15. Verified October 18, 2026. Guidelines change and investor requirements are frequently stricter than the published floor; this is general information, not a commitment to lend.

Why this matters so much

Your debt-to-income ratio is your total monthly obligations divided by your qualifying income. Every dollar of counted student loan payment is a dollar not available for a mortgage payment.

The hard part with student loans is that the payment an underwriter counts frequently is not the payment you actually make. A borrower on an income-driven plan paying a small amount, or paying nothing while deferred, can have a much larger figure used against them depending on how the rule treats their situation. For a borrower with a large balance, the difference between treatments can be the difference between qualifying for the house they want and qualifying for considerably less.

That is why this is worth getting precisely right rather than approximately right, and why the specific terms of your plan matter.

What to bring your lender

Do not describe your student loans from memory. Get the documents, because the treatment depends on specifics:

  • The current statement for every loan, showing the balance and the actual required monthly payment.
  • Your repayment plan type. Standard, graduated, extended, or one of the income-driven plans. Name it specifically.
  • Deferment or forbearance documentation, with the end date, if you are in one.
  • Documentation of any forgiveness program you are enrolled in, including public service, and where you are in it.
  • Evidence of payments actually made, if your plan payment differs from a statement figure.

Then ask your lender directly: "What monthly figure are you using for my student loans, and why that figure?" The answer should cite the rule, not an assumption. If they cannot tell you how they arrived at it, the analysis has not been done.

The limits you are being measured against

What debt-to-income ratio will FHA allow? On a file that receives an automated approval the maximum debt-to-income ratio is whatever the automated underwriting system returns, rather than a single published ceiling. Manually underwritten files must meet HUD's own manual requirements, where the ratio limits are tighter and documented compensating factors are what allow a file above the baseline.

What lenders actually do. The investor layer is where this gets real. The matrix I verified this against requires an internal exception review for any FHA file with a qualifying score below 660 where both ratios exceed 37 and 47. Both have to exceed, so a 39 and 50 goes to review and a 39 and 43 does not. That is an extra approval step most borrowers never hear about, and it is the kind of thing that turns a yes into a maybe. That is my observation from running these files, not a published requirement, and it varies by investor.Source: HUD Handbook 4000.1 Chapter 5, verified against the Qualifying Ratios section of a wholesale FHA guideline current to 2026-10-15. Verified October 18, 2026. Guidelines change and investor requirements are frequently stricter than the published floor; this is general information, not a commitment to lend.

Note that the ratio tolerance differs between the automated evaluation and a manual underwrite, and a manual underwrite applies a tighter framework where compensating factors matter. Which path you are on changes how much room you have. See What a Manual Underwrite Is.

What you can actually do about it

Some of these are real levers and some are traps. In rough order of usefulness:

Get the treatment right first. Before restructuring anything, confirm your lender is applying the rule correctly to your specific plan. A meaningful share of the time the problem is a mis-applied figure rather than a genuine ratio problem. This costs nothing and should always be step one.

Reduce other monthly obligations. Your ratio is a total. A car payment you could pay off, or a card balance you could clear, frees ratio room directly. For many borrowers this is faster and more effective than anything involving the student loans themselves.

Do not take on new debt. Obvious, routinely ignored, and a new car during escrow has ended more approvals than any student loan rule.

Consider a permanent rate buydown. This one is underused. A permanent buydown lowers the note rate, which lowers the qualifying payment, which lowers your ratio. A temporary buydown generally does not, because most programs qualify you at the note rate. If you are a hair over on ratio, a seller concession pointed at a permanent reduction can be the difference between declined and approved. See Permanent vs Temporary Buydown and Seller Concession Limits by Loan Type.

Changing your repayment plan is a real option and needs advice. Moving between plans can change the payment an underwriter counts. It can also change what you pay over the life of the loan, affect forgiveness eligibility, and have tax consequences. Talk to someone qualified on student debt before you do it for mortgage reasons, because optimizing a ratio at the cost of a forgiveness track is a bad trade and I am not the person to advise you on that side of it.

A larger down payment reduces the loan and therefore the payment, which helps the ratio directly.

A co-borrower with income, which solves an income shortfall and brings its own consequences. See Co-Signer or Non-Occupant Co-Borrower.

Can a parent co-sign an FHA loan? A non-occupying co-borrower or cosigner is permitted on an FHA purchase. Cash-out refinancing is not permitted where one is used. The co-borrower may not be an interested party to the sales transaction, which rules out the seller, the builder or the agent.

What lenders actually do. The matrix I verified this against expects overall transaction ratios generally not to exceed 31 and 43 when a non-occupying co-borrower is used, with higher ratios considered where the co-borrower's financial strength is established. That is tighter than the ratios an occupying borrower alone would face, so adding a parent can bring the ceiling down as well as the income up. Worth modelling both ways before anyone signs. That is my observation from running these files, not a published requirement, and it varies by investor.Source: HUD Handbook 4000.1, verified against the Non-Occupant Co-Borrower section of a wholesale FHA guideline current to 2026-10-15. Verified October 18, 2026. Guidelines change and investor requirements are frequently stricter than the published floor; this is general information, not a commitment to lend.

If you are a medical or dental professional

Your situation is different enough to be worth treating separately. Physician and dentist loan programs exist specifically because large student balances and a rising income do not fit standard ratio math, and several of them treat student debt in deferment very differently from FHA.

If you are a resident, a fellow, or early in practice, start there rather than here. See Medical Residents and Student Loans and Physician Home Loans in Scottsdale.

The short version

Get the figure your lender is using, in writing, with the reason. Confirm it against your actual plan. Then work the levers that do not require restructuring your student debt before you consider the ones that do, and get real advice before you change a repayment plan for mortgage reasons.
